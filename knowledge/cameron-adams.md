# Cameron Adams — Co-founder & Chief Product Officer, Canva

> Sources: MDUWwTNDqlQ (First Round Capital), nyc0UiuUfPo (New Economies — this transcript was expected to feature Melanie Perkins but is actually an Adams interview, so it's used here)

## Worldview

- Creativity is fundamentally about difference: "if you're doing exactly the same thing as the person next to you or the founder down the road," you create nothing that attracts customers, investors, or attention. Bringing difference — your brand, your audience, your deep understanding of a field — is the key creator skill in the AI age.
- Taste is not aesthetics; it's "understanding how an audience is going to receive something" and sending the message you want. AI gives you 30 options but no extra chances to send your message — you often get one — so human judgment about which option to ship is the critical differentiator.
- Creativity is a full workflow, not one prompt in a box. It's a journey with feedback, teams, brand, and context that all have to work together — and building that full ecosystem is a much harder problem (and a much deeper moat) than a prompt box that spits out an image.
- First impressions are nearly irreversible: someone coming to your product and deciding it's not for them is what you avoid at all costs, "because trying to win that person back is incredibly hard." This is why Canva went against Lean Startup and launched polished.
- The launch is close to the least important thing on a 10–20 year journey. Exponential charts start at zero; success is in how you turn up every day after — engaging users, responding to feedback, getting them talking.
- You make your own luck by increasing surface area: pushing work out and reaching out to people multiplies your chances of the right collision. "If you just sit in your room at home and never talk to anyone, nothing is going to happen."
- Hold huge vision and a today-useful product simultaneously: build for billions eventually, but zero in on one passionate user group now.

## Frameworks

### Double diamond product discovery
Go wide (explore many concepts — they tried an entirely search-driven interface, different layouts, different text/image manipulation paradigms), converge on a candidate, then go wide again as user response comes in, and finally zero in on the product. Canva spent ~3 months of founder-level prototyping this way before hiring the engineering team, and ~a year total from concept to launch.

### The sketch-to-code iteration loop
Pair design skill with coding skill so a sketch becomes an interactive prototype "within a few hours." The point: you don't know how a product behaves — even for yourself — until you've clicked buttons and dragged things around. You have to feel the product. He notes AI now collapses this further (skip the sketch, go idea → interactive prototype), which turns the product process on its head.

### Do-something onboarding (vs coach marks)
Two steps, derived from watching testers freeze: (1) set expectations fast — a 23-second video of the editor in motion showing what's possible; (2) immediately make them put something on the page themselves — drag out a monkey, put a hat on it, change the hat's color, search for a pizza slice. Explicitly rejects coach-mark tours: users click through the pointers, forget every step, and are left staring at a blank page. The barrier was identity ("I'm not a designer, I'm scared of screwing everything up"), so onboarding's job is to break that identity barrier, not to explain features.

### The user-testing cadence
Before remote tools: put people at the boardroom table and just observe. Then usertesting.com: push a new version every 3–4 days, collect 10–20 recorded sessions, watch where people stall, roll findings straight back into the build. Launch quality was earned this way — "battle tested through user testing" — which is what made ignoring Lean Startup pressure safe rather than arrogant.

### Beachhead persona, applied late
Build the platform broadly from day one (don't pigeonhole a tool meant for everyone), but 2–3 months before launch pick one persona to message to, because "if you push out a product and say this is for everyone, it's really hard to message about." They chose social media managers: time-poor individuals who had to produce lots of visual content, couldn't afford designers or expensive software — and who talked constantly on the very channels they designed for, making them a self-amplifying first audience.

### Community as touchpoints, not forums
Canva never built a destination community. Foster community where it already arises (Facebook groups people create themselves — drop in, share roadmap hints, file their bugs), and treat every touchpoint as community: a frustrated support ticket converted into a fan is another person who'll show a stranger their favorite design at a bus stop. Make users feel like co-builders — when they see the product improve from their feedback, "it creates a real connection... that sort of thing is something money can't buy."

### The SEO jobs-to-be-done engine
Map hundreds/thousands of search terms related to designing something → build landing pages that speak to the searcher's job to be done → give an easy entry point into the editor → make sure the editor visibly completes the job they came for. Put as much craft into that flow as into the core product. Returns showed within 3 months and it remains a massive traffic engine a decade later.

### Monetization by cohort signal
Start with the pricing innovation in the original pitch ($1 elements vs $500 stock photos — cheap ingredients also unlock experimentation). Run it while it grows (30% month-on-month revenue growth), but watch for the cohort that wants more from the product. Build the premium tier from their explicit feedback (Canva for Work: ~four features around brand and running a business), launch it with a real campaign (200k waitlist). Subscription revenue eclipsed the $1-image business within 3 months.

### Profitability as independence
Formed by scar tissue: a lead investor waited until the final signing day of round three, then offered the same money at half the valuation. Canva scrambled, rebuilt the round without them, got better terms — and resolved never to be beholden to any investor or need funds someone else could hold hostage. That decision drove 8+ years of profitability.

### Enterprise is a refounding, not an extension
Grassroots adoption creates a "disorganized and chaotic network graph" inside a company (5,000 individual users inside a 50,000-person org). Wrapping vague promises ("better for large teams, brand consistency") around that got little traction from 2020. It took ~3 years of learning decision makers, brand control, and cross-org needs to build a real enterprise product — which then landed FedEx, NYSE, and Amazon.

## Heuristics & rules of thumb

- Launch polished enough that people trust it and can produce something they're proud of, but quick enough that feedback can still steer you. You can't ship the "all singing all dancing" version on day one.
- If roughly half the people who sign up become ravenous users, the product works — even at 500 users. Fix depth of love before breadth of reach.
- Ship a test build every 3–4 days; 10–20 recorded user sessions per round is enough signal.
- Pick your launch persona 2–3 months before launch; go deep on their use case in the final stretch (they hardened the social-media-graphic case specifically).
- In the early days, focus only on positive, optimistic early adopters; don't spend energy converting naysayers — "you can win the rest of the world over time."
- Expect launch day to be a trickle (they watched single visitors drip into Google Analytics and went to the pub at 11pm); a 15,000-person waitlist built over a year is unremarkable and won't save you.
- Co-founder selection can be fast (2 hours in person + two Skype calls) if you have genuine self-awareness about what you're great at, what you're not, and what the team needs — and a trusted mutual introduction. Complementary-but-overlapping skills: everyone has a locus of genius, everyone can still weigh in everywhere.
- If your home market is small (Australia, 25M people), internationalize as strategy, not afterthought: 8 languages in year 3, 100 the year after; then localization (content, cultural norms), which keeps compounding. Canva's top-5 markets: US, Brazil, Indonesia, India, Philippines.
- Today you build mobile-first, desktop second; Canva's website → iPad (2015) → phone (a year later) sequencing was too slow even for its era.
- Never do a ~2-year full rewrite again — it froze all shipping and all customer responsiveness; it was ultimately the right call (real-time collaboration, scaled teams, velocity) but he's vowed off the pattern.
- Velocity compounds now: AI-augmented teams let Canva ship more in 3 months than any prior period in 14 years — but every launch must still clear the good-first-experience bar.
- Managing AI agents is real work: they fall over, lose connections, need quality checks — "the dream of kicking back on a beach in a deck chair" isn't reality; architecting the framework people and agents slot into is the new skill.

## Red flags they call out

- A product that's "one prompt box that spits an image out" — no workflow, no team, no context, no moat.
- "This is for everyone" at launch — unmessageable, so it reaches no one.
- Coach-mark onboarding that ends with the user alone at a blank page.
- Being beholden to a single investor for survival — someone else can then set your trajectory on signing day.
- Believing day-one-flood launch stories — "most of the stories that you hear about that, they're actually untrue"; the product was usually built two years before you heard of it.
- Sameness: creators and founders doing exactly what the person next to them does, with no difference to stand behind.
- Treating enterprise as a natural upsell of grassroots adoption instead of a distinct product and motion.
- Fully AI-generated content as a strategy: new mediums surge, then settle; people want to connect with real people, and generated content will find niches, not replace authenticity.

## Questions they ask founders

1. What's genuinely different here — what would make anyone's ears perk up versus the founder down the road?
2. Have you put an interactive prototype in someone's hands yet? You don't know how it behaves until they click and drag.
3. What happens in a user's first five minutes — do they actually put something on the page, or do they freeze?
4. In your testing, who got energized? Which specific persona lit up, and are they loud enough to bring others?
5. Can you message this product? If it's "for everyone," who is the beachhead you'll actually speak to at launch?
6. What's your plan for the day after launch — how do you turn up daily, respond to feedback, and turn users into evangelists?
7. What does one passionate user do that brings you the next user? Can you see that loop tangibly happening?
8. Which cohort is straining against the edges of your product, and what package would they pay for?
9. What happens if your next funding round vanishes at signing — can the business survive without putting its hand out?
10. What are you great at, what are you not, and does your team actually cover the gap?
