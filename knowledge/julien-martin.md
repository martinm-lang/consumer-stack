# Julien Martin — Head of Design, Zenly & Amo (French consumer app studio)

> Sources: 4zvNy_CCK7s (Dive Club, interview with Ridd)

## Worldview

- Design differentiation doesn't come from function — "you don't have to build everything to be functional. It can be great to use." The accumulation of tiny interaction details (animations, haptics, feedback) is what makes users perceive quality, even when they can't articulate why.
- A consumer app should have a DNA — a crafted universe/world applied through every pixel and transition, "no randomness." Zenly's all-blue screens, mood messages and crazy ice-cream logo felt like a game and directly drove popularity in strategic markets (Japan, South Korea, Indonesia, Eastern Europe).
- Great product work starts messy and organic, then narrows: go fast in a chaotic exploration space, extract the one essential delightful thing, then "exploit it — go crazy with it."
- Gut feeling is a legitimate design instrument. Starting a new product, you have no data; the starting point is expertise + prior data + intuition. He'd start a product "100% gut feeling" himself, while admitting you then have to watch metrics after release.
- In the AI era the founding designer must be 360: strategy, hands-on craft, brand, and committing their own code. Teams are staying tiny on purpose ("we'll be a billion company… but we'll only be seven of us").

## Frameworks

### The halo effect (find the atom)
Don't design a brand top-down; find one core piece — the atom — whose character radiates into everything.
- At Zenly, the entire design language grew out of just the logo and a first set of bouncy, colorful UI icons.
- At Amo, the atom was the avatar: "it's not an emoji, it's not a bitmoji, it's actually you," animated and expressive — which spawned the fridge-magnet/collage brand (pieces of paper, stickers, photos slapped together, messy but thoughtfully placed) that then drove every app in the suite.
- Application: before styling screens, ask what single element (how a user is represented, one signature interaction) defines the world; derive everything from it.
- Corollary: his first direction for Amo (luxury, onyx-black, gem-like buttons) was killed after 4 months because "it felt nice, but it wasn't funny… it was cold." Polish that isn't warm or fun is the wrong polish.

### Borrow physical/tactile paradigms instead of default UI patterns
The first mechanical answer (panels, carousels, contact-list cells — how Find My and Life360 do it) is the baseline to beat. Amo's Bump browses friends as an iPod-style wheel inspired by rolodexes and old dial phones; friends' faces animate as they hit the center of the screen, creating anticipation and smiles. Like games: infinite games exist, patterns repeat, but the UI/UX can always be new (they also invented new patterns, e.g. Zenly's edge-zoom, which ruins every other map app for you once learned).

### The balance game (delight vs. usability)
New experiences can go too far — people download Amo/Zenly apps and "don't understand what's happening." The discipline: constantly arbitrage "is this delightful enough — but wait, did we lose usability?" Sometimes step back and adjust; sometimes brute-force it because it's your DNA — but if users won't use it or pay for it, you're doing something wrong. Non-negotiable check: word of mouth — does the design make someone screenshot/screen-record it and show it at school ("what's the app?"). Never underestimate passive attraction.

### Side-by-side jamming (anti-tunnel collaboration)
Working alone in a tunnel and presenting finished work is "problematic." Instead: designer and engineer sit together for hours fine-tuning ("can you adjust the speed of this animation, it doesn't feel right") with live previews — no waiting for a shipped build, no closed Figma files. New hires are pushed out of "this is my scope" protectiveness: spend a day with an engineer from another team, lunch, laptops, make it happen. Great ideas came from backend engineers; the whole company — HR included — was product-driven.

## Heuristics & rules of thumb

- The 45-day smile test: "if you're smiling when you use this app even after the 45 days, you know you created something really special."
- For every button ask: what happens when I tap this? What's the reaction, the feedback — a tiny animation, a carefully done haptic, something on screen?
- Double the timebox for craft: if you can build it in two days, spend four — the raised quality bar, even if it's only 2–3% better than a competitor, is worth it. "Who cares about your gradient, just ship it" worked 10 years ago, not today.
- Hiring signal #1 is side projects — "the strongest signal you can get," beating big-company track record. No users, no distribution? Fine. "You were at Meta, you were at Apple, we don't care — talk to us about this very specific side project."
- Speed benchmark for a founding designer: a working prototype in hand 2 days after joining.
- Engineers must be hired onto the product side: willing to sit with design for 5 hours straight fine-tuning things that "might not be important but turn out to be very important."
- ~90% of startup design roles he places want freelance/agency/startup backgrounds with side projects, not big-tech pedigrees.

## Red flags they call out

- Delight as decoration: confetti slapped on at the end of a flow; gamification meaning badges and leaderboards.
- The Christmas tree: feature creep where shiny things and calls-to-action all compete — late Zenly got so overwhelming they had to keep toning it down through repeated redesigns.
- Cold polish: luxurious, tasteful, and joyless. Quality without warmth misses in consumer social.
- Shipping the first mechanical pattern (panels, cells, standard lists) without asking whether a more tactile paradigm exists.
- Novelty past comprehension: users lost, not getting it, and the team refusing to step back.
- "We have to use data" as a veto on new directions when no data can exist yet — "unless you try it, there is no data."
- Designers who tunnel solo and unveil finished work without discussion.
- Big-company candidates who can't show they've shipped anything on their own.

## Questions they ask founders

1. What happens when I tap this button — what's the reaction, the feedback, the haptic?
2. Are your users still smiling at day 45?
3. What's your atom — the one core element your whole universe radiates from?
4. If I stripped the logo, could I still tell this screen is yours? Where's the DNA?
5. Is this delightful, or is it delightful *and* usable? What did you lose?
6. Would a teenager screenshot this and show it at school? What makes someone ask "what's that app?"
7. Why did you use the default pattern here — what's the tactile, physical version of this interaction?
8. You built this in two days; what would it look like if you'd spent four?
9. What have you shipped on your own, outside work, in the last six months?
10. Is it fun — or just nice?
