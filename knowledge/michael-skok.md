# Michael Skok — Venture investor; creator of "Startup Secrets" at Harvard Innovation Labs

> Sources: q8d9uuO1Cf4 (Harvard Innovation Labs — "Value Props: Create a Product People Will Actually Buy" workshop). Speaker unnamed in captions; identified as Michael J. Skok with high confidence — the session matches his "Startup Secrets: Building a Value Proposition" talk at Harvard i-lab (the value-prop template and the Unworkable/Unavoidable/Urgent/Underserved framework are both his named, published frameworks from that series).

## Worldview

- The number one reason companies fail is that they're not solving a valuable enough problem — so they never create value worth investing in.
- Ideas are worthless in themselves: "two a penny," free-floating until they address a specific problem or opportunity for a specific who. He wants to "get rid of the idea of ideas."
- Everything must be looked at through the user's eyes, not yours. The customer's answers — not your pitch — define the problem, the urgency, and the pain of adoption.
- If your target customer is "everybody," you will fail by default; even the largest companies in the world get very crisp about segments.
- A great value prop is necessary but not sufficient: Rent the Runway nailed the value prop and still struggled because the business model was tough. Value prop and viable business model have to work together.
- The best startups are built around the founder ("founder/market fit"): if you don't uniquely understand the problem, someone who does will out-compete you.

## Frameworks

### The value prop statement
The workshop's one "answer," assembled from all the other frameworks: **For (target segment) who is dissatisfied with (current alternative) due to (unmet need), we offer a (product/service) that solves (that need), providing (key benefits) compelling enough that people actually buy.** Every slot must be filled specifically; the most common fatal omission is the "for who."

### Minimum Viable Segment (MVS)
The dance partner of the MVP. Find one path where the same users have the same needs, so you can sell the same product over and over without changing it. Without an MVS, the whole-world market pulls your MVP in every direction at once. Test: can the user identify with the product? Ask them — keep asking until you find the person who says "that's the problem I have."

### User vs. customer
When the payer isn't the user, you need two value propositions — one for the funder, one for the user — but always start with the user: without user pull, nobody pays. The user is what convinces the gatekeeper there's a payoff.

### The 4U problem test
A problem worth solving is some combination of:
- **Unworkable**: consequences so great somebody gets fired (iPhone launch activation failures) — or, for social problems, people stay sick, uneducated, or in unrest.
- **Unavoidable**: like taxes, death/aging, education, or a pandemic — realities that spawn whole industries.
- **Urgent**: it's the buyer's number-one priority right now. Urgency is relative and competes with finite time, money, and people.
- **Underserved**: real whitespace — e.g., menopause gets ~2% of relevant research funding; Kenyan coffee consumers can only buy Brazilian instant.
Keep drilling to root cause (is it lack of awareness or lack of access?) by asking the user, not hypothesizing.

### Latent/aspirational vs. blatant/critical (B2C)
Consumer products rarely start "unworkable"; they usually address latent, aspirational needs (look good, feel connected). The 2x2: latent↔blatant, aspirational↔critical. You want to reach blatant + critical — the must-have quadrant. Products migrate: the iPad was "a big iPhone" until pilots and surgeons made it mission-critical via apps. Facebook wins on the deepest need — connection (loneliness being the #1 driver of unhappiness in Harvard's happiness study). Map where your use case sits and how it moves up and right.

### Whole product & dependencies
Your product is almost never the whole solution: a smartphone is a brick without carriers and apps; Tesla depends on charging infrastructure. List the external dependencies required for your product to solve the problem end to end — or you're selling something that can't actually solve it. Platforms (iPad + developers, open source, AWS) are the classic way to let others complete your product.

### The 3Ds instead of faster-better-cheaper
Faster/better/cheaper is dangerous: incumbents have more resources and can out-execute you on all three. Cut through the noise with a 3D breakthrough:
- **Disruptive**: often a business model, not a technology (Airbnb owned nothing; Google gave software away on ads while Microsoft charged).
- **Discontinuous**: enables what simply couldn't be done before (multitouch, AWS/cloud).
- **Defensible**: IP, switching costs, network effects, data moats, long-term contracts (10-year farmer contracts for a consumer coffee brand).

### Before/after T-diagram
State the situation before your product and after: "acute pain or absolute joy." Push to the extreme, and get the customer to enumerate both lists themselves. You want penicillin/morphine, not a vitamin.

### Gain/Pain ratio
Measure the gain you deliver against the total pain of adoption: finding you, trying, buying, training, integration, plus the middle band of risk and inertia (will this startup even exist next year? cold-start network problems). Founders can always list the gain; they almost never list the pain. Enterprise rule of thumb: ~10:1 gain-to-pain to overcome activation energy; consumers may switch for less (maybe 10–20% cheaper) — measure the threshold with real users. Easy adoption cuts both ways: point-click download, point-click delete.

## Heuristics & rules of thumb

- "Don't pitch them anything, ask them everything." Everything in the value prop is discovered by asking users, never by pitching.
- Urgency test: ask a prospect their number-one priority tomorrow morning, then "when do you think you'll get to number two?" If you're not in the answer, expect to be deprioritized indefinitely.
- Objection mining: ask "what are all the reasons you would not buy my product?" over and over until they've told you every pain point.
- A problem well stated is half solved — if the team can't crisply agree what problem is being solved for whom, five engineers will build five things.
- Ride market shifts that create urgency (mobile forced banking online; AI is forcing every process to be rethought now).
- Claim order-of-magnitude, quantified improvement: "you don't just say it's X, you say it's 10x and here's why" — otherwise the customer rationally does nothing.
- Show data behind the unworkable claim (e.g., "20% of students won't graduate" — public data you can cite).
- Be willing to conclude your idea isn't a startup at all — some problems should be solved by policy or government; recognizing that honestly is an "aha," not a failure.

## Red flags they call out

- "It's for everybody" — no identified who, no persona, no way to pick a go-to-market.
- Pitching "faster better cheaper" as the differentiation — incumbents will crush it with resources.
- Founders who can list every gain and none of the adoption pain.
- Hypothesizing the problem (awareness vs. access) instead of drilling to root cause with users.
- Nice-to-have vitamins pitched as if they were must-have painkillers, with no story for how the latent need becomes critical.
- Ignoring dependencies — selling something that can't solve the problem end to end.
- A compelling value prop bolted to an unsustainable business model (the Rent the Runway trap).
- Solving a problem the founder has no unique understanding of.

## Questions they ask founders

- For who, exactly? Not everybody — who is the persona, and can they identify with the product?
- Is the user the customer? If not, what's the value prop for each?
- Which of the 4Us does your problem hit — and what's your evidence from users, not your hypothesis?
- What happens if nobody solves this problem? What does "before" really cost?
- What's your prospect's number-one priority tomorrow morning — and are you it?
- What are all the reasons they would NOT buy?
- What's disruptive, discontinuous, and defensible here — beyond faster, better, cheaper?
- What are the dependencies your product needs to be a whole solution?
- Is your gain/pain ratio an order of magnitude, and how did you measure the customer's switching threshold?
- Why you? What do you uniquely understand about this problem?
