# Dylan Field — Co-founder & CEO, Figma

> Sources: WyJV6VwEGA8 (Lenny's Podcast — craft/quality as moat, Figma Make, multi-product strategy)

## Worldview

- Design is now how software companies win or lose. The era of "good enough" is over — when anyone can generate lots of code fast, value moves up the stack and craft becomes the differentiator.
- TAM is the wrong filter for ideas. At Figma's start the Bureau of Labor Statistics counted ~250,000 designers in the world; the trend (rising design expectations across all software) mattered more than the point-in-time market size, and the market expanded to meet the product.
- An MVP is not enough. Users need to see the vision of where you're going in the very first release — something at least "a little bit awesome" — or they won't come along.
- AI output is "the realm of law of averages": a starting point, never the finished thing. The differentiation is in helping people get to a great starting point, iterate toward excellent, and explore the option space wide.
- Removing blockers is as important for retention as shipping cool new stuff — but if you only do table-stakes features you don't have an awesome product. You need both.
- Roles are merging: designers, engineers, PMs, researchers are all becoming "product builders" who specialize in one area but do many things. You have to believe your organization gets better as models get better.

## Frameworks

### Time to value
Get a new user to experience something special about the product as fast as possible. In Figma design: get them from blank canvas to creating something, then to a collaborative multiplayer moment. In Figma Make: prompt to an awesome result quickly. Every product review should ask: what is the incredible moment, and how many steps stand between signup and it?

### The blockers team
Figma at one point had a literal team called "blockers" whose only job was striking down adoption blockers one by one. Each blocker removed produced a visible improvement in activation and retention — "you could literally see the change in the graph." The discipline: treat unglamorous blocking-and-tackling as a first-class workstream alongside innovative features, because a blocked user never experiences your value at all.

### Follow the workflow (product expansion)
New product lines come from tracing the user's end-to-end journey: idea → brainstorm (FigJam) → design (Figma Design) → code (Dev Mode) → publish (Sites). Watch what people already do inside your existing product that would be better pulled out into its own surface — slides were being made in Figma Design, so Figma Slides; whiteboarding was happening there, so FigJam; brand/marketing bulk-asset work, so Buzz. Expansion is "strength to strength," not sorting a spreadsheet by next-biggest TAM.

### One-to-two is the hard product jump
Going from one product to two is much harder than two to n. The second product (FigJam) was controversial internally, driven by years of intuition before the reasoning existed, and only became "obviously we should do this" when COVID spiked the use case. Shipping it built the organizational muscle to keep expanding. Expect the second product to feel wrong to the team; that's not a reason to skip it, but you need real user-signal before sprinting.

### Fun as a differentiator (context-dependent)
A month before FigJam's launch: "we built the thing… the soul isn't there." The board-level decision was to differentiate by making it fun — a one-day design sprint produced ~20 ideas, several (like cursor chat) became definitional. But fun is contextual: in a brainstorming tool it draws out withdrawn people; in a production design tool a quirky thing in your way is annoying. Ask what emotional job the surface does before adding play.

### The taste loop
Taste = your point of view and how you develop it. The loop: have an experience → do I like it? why? → learn the canon and context that led to it → decide where you agree or disagree philosophically → repeat across mediums and fields. Almost anyone can match a well-articulated taste framework; very few can create one — so leaders should create and articulate the framework ("what is taste for us") rather than expect everyone to be a tastemaker. Cross-discipline exposure (his examples are musicians turned designers) strengthens it.

## Heuristics & rules of thumb

- Figma took 5 years from founding (Aug 2012) to first revenue (summer 2017), with closed beta only in Dec 2015. His verdict: don't do that — get to market faster; the lesson is NOT "sweat every detail and never ship."
- If a project isn't converging or keeps dragging out, be willing to kill it and move people to other projects.
- Interrogate timeline estimates from first principles: separate real constraints from well-intentioned padding. Come from curiosity — sometimes you're missing a constraint, sometimes the team is under-staffed, sometimes assumptions are just wrong.
- Map people to the projects they're genuinely fired up about; they'll "outperform your wildest imagination." The same person on the wrong project is merely fine.
- Prototypes beat static mocks; static mocks beat lots of words. Bring a prototype into the conversation, not just a PRD.
- Generated output must match the team's design system, or good ideas get filtered out for looking wrong — consistency lets an idea be evaluated on its merits.
- If an AI feature failure was preventable by QA (his Make Designs / weather-app incident), pull the feature. Vibes-based quality assessment "will get you somewhere, but it's not rigorous" — invest in evals.
- When leading through a crisis (the collapsed Adobe deal): communicate on a drumbeat, narrow expectations early ("the path is narrowing"), then offer an explicit exit (the "detach" program: 3 months severance, ~4% took it) so everyone remaining is bought in on the pace.
- Clarity is the leader's recurring job: when an area feels murky, either you haven't done the work to understand it or nobody has. Push until "we know what we're trading off" and everyone's on the same page, even if not everyone agrees.
- Treat all user feedback as a gift — he answers Figma bug complaints on X even on IPO day.

## Red flags they call out

- "Good enough" as a quality bar — he calls it mediocre; you have to get to great, preferably excellent, to win.
- Shipping the bare MVP with no awesome moment and no communicated vision.
- Choosing what to build by sorting on TAM instead of trend and strength-to-strength fit.
- Rah-rah forced positivity in place of hard conversations — "it's not about positive or negative, it's about: do we understand it?"
- Teams trusting vibes instead of rigorous evals for AI products; not putting a wide-surface AI feature through its paces before launch.
- Padding in schedules that nobody has reasoned through from first principles.
- Assuming a model can do more than it can — "you're going to have a bad time"; know where capabilities actually lie.
- Treating AI purely as cost-cutting instead of as a way to grow and do more.

## Questions they ask founders

- What's the incredible moment in your product, and how fast does a new user reach it? What's your time to value?
- What's blocking users from adopting at all — and why are you shipping new features before striking those blockers down?
- Is this good enough, or is it actually great? Where does craft show up in this product?
- What do users already do inside your product that wants to be its own surface?
- What's the trend that expands your market, given the TAM data says it's small?
- Is it really going to take this long? What are the assumptions, and what's padding?
- Have you had the hard conversations? What are we actually trading off here?
- Does this thing have soul, or is it just built?
- How are you QA-ing and eval-ing this, beyond vibes?
- Where do you disagree with the canon — what's your point of view?
