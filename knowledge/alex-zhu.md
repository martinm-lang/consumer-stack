# Alex Zhu — Co-founder, Musical.ly (later TikTok)

> Sources: ey15v81pwII (TechCrunch Disrupt, on-stage interview with Josh Constine)

## Worldview

- The most important asset of a social network is not the content — it's the social graph. Content trends come and go; the graph of connected real-world friends is what compounds and what you build everything else on.
- Consumer social apps win by lowering the barrier to content creation until everyone can be an entertainer. Musical.ly positioned itself as an "entertainment social network" — distinct from Instagram and Snapchat — where ordinary people produce entertaining content straight from their phone.
- Creators don't leave a platform because they're unhappy; they leave because the consumers left. Sustained consumer engagement is what retains creators, not creator perks.
- Competition from giants cloning you is a permanent fact of startup life. You can't plan around "what if Snapchat copies us" — the only response is to keep making the product better and keep innovating.
- As a platform's audience broadens (parents, teachers), the feed must become personalized per user, or older users' presence kills the product's coolness for the young core.

## Frameworks

### Brush → Canvas
His model for platform evolution:
- **Zero to one: be a brush.** Be extremely specific. Solve one narrow need exceptionally well. For Musical.ly this was lip-syncing.
- **One to X: be a canvas.** Once you've hit scale (for Musical.ly: #1 on App Store downloads), deliberately diversify content and use cases so "all kinds of things happen on this blank canvas" — comedy, sports, fashion, makeup, live streaming.
- Application: identify which phase a product is in. In brush phase, reject generality. In canvas phase, actively push content diversification and watch whether the original format's share of content is declining (for Musical.ly, lip-sync/music content share falling over time was a *good* sign).

### Lower the barrier to creation
Why lip-syncing beat karaoke as a seed format — a two-part test for any UGC format:
1. **Can everyone do it, and does everyone's output look good?** Only ~10% of people can sing karaoke well enough to want to share it. With lip-syncing, everyone can do it and "everyone thinks the videos are perfect."
2. **Is the output visually diverse?** Karaoke videos are all a person singing — visually boring, no diversity. Lip-sync supports comedy, dance, many formats.
A format that fails either test can't seed a UGC community.

### Consumer-first retention (the anti-Vine model)
The key difference from Vine: on Musical.ly the consumers *are* the creators, because creation is easy. So don't build a creator-appeasement program — build a product model that retains consumers, and creator retention follows from their engagement.

### Marketing machine via daily challenges
Musical.ly ran a daily challenge generating over 1 million videos per day. Challenges are a repeatable demand-generation mechanic — and can be pointed at brand/social goals (e.g. the "Don't Judge" anti-bullying challenge). Parents even requested challenges via direct WeChat lines.

## Heuristics & rules of thumb

- 25% of daily active users being content creators is an "extremely high ratio" — his benchmark for a healthy creation-driven network. High creator ratio → creators connect with each other → social graph forms.
- Watch the content mix over time: if the founding format's share is shrinking while original/new formats grow, the canvas transition is working.
- Real-world friend density matters: once user penetration in a market is high enough that people find their actual friends on the app, the graph becomes defensible.
- Trust & safety response time: a reported underage account gets removed within 10–15 minutes of a parent complaint.
- When a giant clones you, expect the clone to succeed at the feature level (he conceded Instagram Stories–style success was plausible for a Snapchat lip-sync clone) — your only lever is product velocity.

## Red flags they call out

- UGC formats with a high skill barrier (karaoke): most users can't produce shareable output, so the creation flywheel never starts.
- Visually monotonous content formats: no diversity in the feed → boring community.
- Making "keep the creators happy" the strategy instead of keeping consumers engaged.
- Staying a brush too long — refusing to diversify once you've won the initial use case ("musically has to evolve into a more generic platform").
- Freezing up because an incumbent might copy you: "we can't say hey because Snapchat is going to build a copycat we don't do anything anymore."
- One-size-fits-all feeds as the audience ages up — without personalization, parents and teachers in the feed make the app uncool for teens.

## Questions they ask founders

1. Is your product a brush or a canvas right now — and are you being specific enough for the phase you're in?
2. Can *everyone* create on your platform, and does everyone's output look good to them?
3. Is the content your users produce visually diverse, or will the feed all look the same?
4. What percent of your DAU are creators? How do those creators connect to each other?
5. Do your users have their real-world friends on the app yet, or is the graph interest-only?
6. When consumers get bored, what keeps them? Don't tell me about creator incentives — tell me about consumer engagement.
7. What happens to you when Instagram or Snapchat ships this as a filter?
8. What repeatable mechanic (like a daily challenge) generates demand for creation every single day?
9. As your audience broadens, how does the experience stay different for a 14-year-old vs. their parent?
