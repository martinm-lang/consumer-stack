# Antoine Martin — Co-founder & CEO, Zenly

> Sources: l5dFuyyOY7A (Startupfood, on-stage interview + audience Q&A, Paris)

## Worldview

- Retention comes first, growth is a consequence: "if you have retention you will have growth." Don't even look at growth until roughly two years after first launch; the natural founder instinct of growth-first, retention-second "most often doesn't lead to successes."
- Purpose before product: be very clear on the core product primitive, the value you bring, and the core narrative *before* building — he sees French startups repeatedly grow before understanding their own primitives.
- In social, users hire the product for a second-degree job. Nobody opens Zenly to see a pin on a map; they open it because friends showed up at their club on a Saturday out of nowhere. Successful social products understand and amplify these second-degree implications, and provoke emotion through lots of little delights (his explanation of why teens idolize Snap).
- Social consumer at scale requires a product/design CEO: "I still haven't met a very successful company [at that scale] that isn't led by a CEO who is very product or design" — and companies (Twitter, Facebook) visibly go the wrong way when the vision owner is defocused, and recover when they return.
- The app-boom era is over; the market is mature and saturated. Product alone is no longer enough — brand, marketing, and distribution are now essential (TikTok imposed a global social product through spending, exactly like a Coca-Cola CMO launching a soda). This disadvantages the older generation of product-only companies and creates a new wave of opportunity.
- User data belongs to the user: "we've never sold user data, we'll never sell user data." Not a marketing argument — a internal ground rule so the team makes the right engineering/product calls whenever user benefit conflicts with company objective.
- Resilience is the real differentiator between founders who make it and those who don't: the only thing blocking success is how many tries you've had, provided you learn fast each time.

## Frameworks

### Purpose → retention → engagement → growth (in that order)
His canonical sequence: (1) get a clear proposition — why does this product exist, what benefit for the user; (2) build and measure retention, ignoring growth (~2 years post-launch); (3) drive engagement — for Zenly, good reasons to open the app more often; (4) only optimize growth when you're already very big. Growth pursued out of order is the most common failure pattern he sees.

### Frequency, not time spent
Zenly refuses to optimize time-in-app — no ads means no good reason to hold you. Session length is a *quality* metric inverted: "if your session is 20 seconds long, we're doing an okay job; if your session is 40 seconds long, it's probably that we have an engineering problem." The KPI is opens per day — being useful at many different moments — which also compounds virally: five visible opens during a conference makes neighbors ask what the app is.

### The first two minutes (motivational waves)
Activation is ~80% of the retention job and it happens in the user's first few hours — really the first two minutes: "if within two minutes these actions aren't done, we're out." Mechanics:
- Users ride motivational waves: peak motivation at download, decaying within ~10 seconds (bad Wi-Fi, distraction). The onboarding's first job is to pump motivation back up.
- Stack required inputs early (location permission, first name, phone verify), one required action per screen — contrary to online advice, *more* screens with simpler steps.
- Activation includes what's outside the app: the App Store page and the text messages users send each other set expectations that drive the right onboarding actions.
- Then friending: for a social network, getting one or several friends (network-dependent) is the #1 success factor.

### McDonald's Wednesdays (field testing under real distraction)
Weekly ritual since the early days: go to a fast-food chain full of students, pay for a teen's Big Mac and wait in line for them; in exchange they test the app for 15 minutes while being filmed. Crucially done *there*, not in the office — during lunch the kid gets snaps every 20 seconds, switches between 20 apps, and the onboarding must survive that. "Seems very simple, very stupid — to this day probably one of our most important secrets." When iOS made always-on location harder: "we just go more often to McDonald's."

### Signals build conviction; never implement user requests directly
"I don't think we've ever implemented directly something coming from a user — if that happens we've been very bad, because it means it's obvious." User feedback is a signal pointing at something else. Collect signals obsessively (he personally Google-translated Japanese tweets 300 times a day), build conviction from them, and when conviction and users conflict, conviction wins.

### Freelancers-first team building
Hire freelancers before employees: they let a broke startup work with people who'd never answer a job offer and could join Google tomorrow; you watch how different people react to real problems before committing ("hiring full-time first is like proposing marriage on the first date"). 100% of Zenly's current leaders started as freelancers — including a VP Engineering courted for 5 years, brought in for one week of consulting, converted over 6–8 months. Also his fix for France's 3-month notice period making mis-hires cost six months.

### No product managers (until ~50 people)
Deliberately no PMs: nobody between engineers and designers forces the whole company toward product thinking. Cycle: a designer hears something from a user in the morning, designs before lunch, rebounds with an engineer, ships and iterates. Result: engineers more product-driven and designers more engineering-aware than anywhere else. He concedes PMs exist for a reason and become necessary as you grow past ~50.

### Non-technical CEO as an advantage
A CEO who can't code is forced to become the best at finding resources (fundraising, pitch, hiring) — and can't micro-arbitrate technical decisions, which pushes ownership down, yielding better decisions and faster learning. Of ~200 location-sharing startups he's watched, the engineer-founded ones struggle to sell the long-term vision or hire a great team.

## Heuristics & rules of thumb

- DAU and its growth rate is the one KPI on the office dashboards since day one. Paul Graham's YC bar is 7% weekly growth; Zenly has always aimed for 6% per week.
- Activation: ~80% of the retention job happens in the user's first few hours; the required actions must complete within the first two minutes.
- Onboarding screens: one required action per screen; more screens with simple steps beat fewer dense ones.
- Social retention benchmark (from the host, endorsed on stage): ~5 active friends → strong retention.
- Latency is retention: friend location must render in ~500ms; at 2–3 seconds users lose the habit and revert to texting. Any infra latency shows up as a usage flattening.
- Spend time with real users every week, in their environment, filming everything.
- Pick KPIs native to your category — opens/day makes sense for Zenly, would be nonsense for Uber; benchmarks in social are rarely public, but founders will tell you off the record if you just ask (Kevin at Instagram, Evan at Snap answer).
- Early fundraising recipe today: find a clear product primitive and narrative, launch, and if retention is good the money follows; monetizing early via subscriptions is now viable (Mojo, Yubo) in a way it wasn't five years ago.
- Stay headquartered where talent is accessible (Paris), but fly to the Bay often — weeks at a time — for ambition, benchmarks and network. Being *based* in the Bay now takes ~$15M just to field a good team.
- MVP truth test: build the first version and you'll know very soon — his signal was arriving at a birthday party and collecting 10 bug reports before reaching the birthday boy, from friends who were forbidden to have the app.

## Red flags they call out

- Optimizing growth before retention, or retention before purpose.
- Optimizing time spent in a social app that claims to serve users.
- Implementing user feature requests literally.
- Copying what you read about hot startups: "be very careful what you see in the news" — reported numbers are often inflated (his read on tbh), growth hacks hide behind the curtain, and growth without long-term purpose kills the product months after the exit.
- Growth before understanding your own core product primitives — the recurring French-ecosystem failure he cites.
- Engineer/CEO founders in social who can't sell a long-term vision or attract a great team.
- Believing product quality alone still wins distribution in a saturated app market.
- Moving a baby startup to the Bay Area instead of faking presence with frequent trips.

## Questions they ask founders

1. What is the reason for your product's existence — what benefit are you giving users, in one clear narrative?
2. What's your retention curve? Why are you showing me growth?
3. What has to happen in the user's first two minutes, and how does your onboarding rebuild motivation after the download dip?
4. How many friends does a user need before they retain — and how do you get them there?
5. What's the second-degree job your product is hired for? What emotion does it provoke?
6. How many times a day do users open it — and are you optimizing frequency or time spent?
7. When did you last watch a real user onboard outside your office, with all their distractions?
8. What signal is this user request actually pointing at?
9. Would your product survive 2–3 seconds of latency? Do you know where the habit breaks?
10. Who on your team could work at Google tomorrow, and how did you get them?
