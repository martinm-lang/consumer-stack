# Kevin Systrom — Co-founder & CEO, Instagram

> Sources: 3pvpNKUPbIY (Lex Fridman #243), 5x912QLs5uY (Tim Ferriss Show), Y9FzqYpR6iE (Startup Grind, onstage with Samir Kaul — Systrom's remarks only), bglIiiGj8Bs (James Jani documentary — embedded Systrom/Krieger clips only), giaL_SPUt7c (20VC clip)

## Worldview

- The biggest companies are founded when enormous technical shifts happen. Instagram existed because
  the iPhone 4 put a real camera with a network attached in every pocket; the founding question was
  "what will change because everyone has a camera in their pocket?"
- Solve a problem, always. "The way we set ourselves apart from the crowd was focusing on problems" —
  most entrepreneurs are technology in search of a problem. If stuff doesn't exist and the experience
  sucks, that's what you should go build.
- Simplicity wins. Instagram's value #1 was "community first," #2 was "do the simple thing first."
  People judge management by the complexity of its decisions; the right call is usually the simplest one.
- People are wired to avoid conflict, so they will not tell you the truth about your product — but
  usage will. Time is people's currency; if they don't spend it on you, it's because the product
  isn't great, whatever they politely say.
- Most successful things are pivots (YouTube was a dating site; Twitter came out of Odeo; Google is
  the lone counterexample he grants). Since you'll probably be wrong, the winning skill is being
  equipped to fail fast and redirect before the money runs out.
- Users ascribe how a product makes them feel to the founder. Companies that keep shipping hits
  people love keep the public on their side; companies that stop shipping joy get picked apart.
- Great outcomes require working roughly "three standard deviations above the mean." Nobody has to
  sign up for that — but if you join the Yankees, you do batting practice.
- Love the outcome you're shooting for, not the everyday. Work is hard and often miserable; passion
  for the mission is what survives the days that suck — and what gets you back up when knocked down.

## Frameworks

### Pivot by subtraction
Instagram wasn't a new idea bolted onto Burbn — "we got to the idea by taking away features."
The procedure:
1. List the 2–3 things users *uniquely* love about your product that no other product gives them
   (for Burbn: posting a photo of what you're doing right now).
2. Ask the users who stayed why they stayed — never why the others left.
3. Cross off anything crowded or boring ("me-too" check-ins, planners, group chat).
4. Circle the one greenfield item that's also a big need — everything else felt crowded; photos didn't.
5. Cut every other feature and rebuild around the circled thing alone.

### What exists / what sucks
His idea-generation test: look out at the world and ask "what exists, and what sucks?"
For mobile photos in 2010 the sucks-list was:
- Photos looked like crap → filters that lean into the flaw and make it a style (the Holga insight:
  make them "suck more, but in an artistic way").
- Uploads took 30–40 seconds → background upload so it *feels* instant.
- Sharing to several networks meant repeating work in every app → one-tap cross-posting.
Each pain point becomes a pillar. A product is the ordered list of pain points it kills — not a
vision statement. The filter idea itself came from one honest user (his wife: "I won't use it,
my photos aren't good") — the blocker to sharing was the real problem to solve.

### Startup as learning rate
He describes company-building in gradient-descent terms:
- Launch to ~100 people, look at what they actually do, measure how wrong you are, nudge the
  company in that direction, repeat quickly.
- Companies fail in two ways: learning rate too slow (adamant they're right while the data says
  otherwise) or too high (wildly chasing new ideas, never settling into a groove).
- Product review, in his style: "what did the data say, and how did we adjust?"
- Think beyond the greedy single session: ask which sequence of experiences correlates with someone
  still being around next week and next month, not just whether one photo loaded fast.

### Jobs to be done (via Christensen's *Competing Against Luck*)
Every product in a user's life is an employee they hired for a role.
- Instagram's job: sharing your life with others, visually — feel connected, show off, feel cared about.
- Features succeed when they serve the same parent job *better*: Stories removed the fear of
  permanence and huge audiences, so it outperformed the feed at the original job.
- Features fail when they hang off an unrelated job: IGTV "probably didn't" serve it; shopping was
  "completely unrelated."
- Facebook's chronic error, in his telling: copying shiny objects instead of understanding the job
  of its own core product — users end up asking "why do I even open this app?"

### Single-player mode
A social product must be worth using with zero friends on it.
- Instagram masqueraded as a filter app, so it "totally worked as a single-player game"; many users
  didn't realize it was a social network until a friend showed up — and then the network kicked in.
- Pair this with deliberately seeding the community: a small group of people producing content
  everyone wants to see (the early photographers who became the first instagrammers).
- The restaurant test: it doesn't matter how good the food is if you show up and you're the only one there.

### Hide the latency where it matters
You don't need to be fast; you need to *feel* fast at the moment the user cares.
- Pre-upload the photo in the background while the user types a caption (trick lifted from Gmail
  loading your account the moment you type your username).
- Picking 512×512 meant filters rendered in ~3 seconds instead of the competitors' 20 — shrink the
  problem instead of optimizing the solution.
- They even animated the progress bar slowly enough that "done" felt earned: "we were slow, we just
  hid the slowness," and users called it the fastest upload they'd ever had.
- Corollary: as long as the product outcome is right, users forgive almost everything else — then
  you compress the latency for real over time.

### Decisions as inventory (via Goldratt's *The Goal*)
A company is a factory whose raw material is ideas; they flow through machines (idea → mock →
draft → beta → shipped).
- The slowest machine gates the whole line — and it is usually leadership decision-making.
- At Instagram he and Krieger kept a doc listing the "inventory of decisions" stacked up waiting,
  then held sessions that did nothing but work through the stack.
- Diagnostic: if mocks and ideas are piling up, the bottleneck (the Herbie) is you.

### The three circles
Before starting anything, draw three circles and build only in the overlap:
1. What do I have real experience in? (Be honest — look at your actual track record.)
2. What am I excited enough about to survive the painful parts?
3. What does the world need? (If this circle is missing, nothing else matters — plenty of startups
   exist that no one needs.)
He calls himself a strict rationalist about circle 3: "just because you think it's cool doesn't
mean it's a big business opportunity — what evidence do you have?"

### Prove yourself wrong
"Knowing that you're right is working as hard as you can to prove that you're wrong. If you can't,
there's only one option left — you're probably right."
- Assume every idea you have is wrong; pressure-test with people who will actually push back.
- There is no Midas touch and no "product guy": successful people have tons of bad ideas and win by
  editing them out; the failures just get written out of history.
- Dig for negative feedback explicitly — people default to "everything was great" (his Thanksgiving
  test: he cooked, knew he'd screwed things up, and nobody would say so).

## Heuristics & rules of thumb

- Ask why the users who *keep* using it keep using it — never why the others don't. The whole
  Instagram pivot came from ~100 Burbn users all loving one feature.
- Product-market fit is cohorts and trends, not an absolute number: ~1,000 signups/week with
  retention curves that hold — and older cohorts curving back *up* as friends join — beats any
  magic 10k/50k threshold.
- Ask people for feedback, then trust the data over their words: track engagement, days-per-week,
  impressions per day from day one, even with 50 beta users. "Data doesn't lie."
- But never fly on one metric — that's flying a plane staring only at airspeed. Keep a scan going
  and synthesize several.
- Don't touch scaling before product-market fit. Duct-tape it. The default startup death is
  over-engineering for scale at 100 users; the "we grew too fast" death is rare and solvable.
- Do the simple thing first — canonical story: 4 months rewriting for a fancy auto-sharding
  database vs. the ~2 hours it took Krieger to write the odd/even user-ID sharding file that just worked.
- Write tests from the beginning: go slow to go fast. Even at 4 people it let anyone touch any part
  of the product without bringing the site down.
- Expect a ~1% hit rate on ideas — the best quant funds bat 50.001% and win on volume. Maximize
  at-bats, edit hard, and admit misses out loud (he did, internally, as CEO).
- Launch fast as a forcing function: Instagram v1 was ~8 weeks of work by two non-professional iOS
  developers. Pressing the button is what forced them to learn everything else.
- Compete only where the incumbent structurally cannot pivot — Instagram was mobile-only and
  visual-only while Facebook sat on desktop and Twitter refused inline images. Never attack the
  core business head-on; look for the cracks. The openings recur every 3–5 years (Instagram →
  Snapchat → TikTok), so "it's too late" is usually wrong.
- Timing is everything and it's the hardest element to predict: Odeo was a podcast directory that
  was simply early. Ask what shift makes *now* the moment.
- Raising money is hiring someone for your team, not taking cash. He picked investors who had
  scaled large networks (Kohler, D'Angelo, Sacca, Dorsey) specifically to reuse their playbook —
  and warns that misaligned investors mean noisy boardrooms and fights.
- If you sell, sell only to a rocket ship — the test he and Krieger applied to Facebook: "can this
  thing reach a higher potential, more quickly, inside?"
- Hire up, on purpose: hire people so good you'd work for them someday, then manage vision and
  direction and ask *them* how to get there. "If everyone hired up, you'd have the most badass
  company in the world."
- The higher you rise, the less honest feedback you get — dig for it, ask for the negative
  explicitly, and thank whoever delivers it so they do it again. (The one hire who watched two
  years of his interviews and told him to smile more and slow down earned lasting gratitude.)
- Have an inkling of the business model even if you defer it: for a social product that's ads, and
  that's fine — but "I have no idea how this makes money" is not a fun position.
- Know your own motivators (he took Bridgewater's personality test to learn "what my API was"):
  he scores low on competition, high on self-imposed challenge — staying 6 years post-acquisition
  was about proving Instagram wasn't done.
- Beware "we have arrived" syndrome after any win: the sale felt like the end to everyone else;
  the next six years (13 → 1,000+ people, 50M → 1B users, zero → real revenue) were the actual company.

## Red flags they call out

- Technology in search of a problem; founding a company just to found a company. "Ideas are not
  companies. Ideas are not products."
- Pitches leaning on fad words — "...and it uses AI," or crypto — with no clear problem being
  solved for the counterparty behind them.
- Problem-shaped holes: the problem is already solved well; too few people actually have it; or the
  pitch misunderstands (or never asked) what people need.
- Over-engineering before anything breaks; scaling infrastructure for a billion users at 100 users;
  equating complicated decisions with good management.
- Sticking with a dying idea out of ego instead of diagnosing and pivoting — "far too many people,
  because of ego or whatever, stick with ideas far too long."
- Confusing your passion for a market — no evidence the world needs it. Passion is necessary
  (people talking founders out of their passion is "a crime") but never sufficient.
- Faking productivity: people who "convince themselves they work really hard" — boards, calls, the
  show of it — versus operators with their ass on the line. He likes burning bridges so failure isn't an option.
- Answering "work smart, not hard": in his experience those people usually don't yet know what
  working smart means; it's laziness or ego talking. The humble answer is "hard" or "both."
- Going it alone. No co-founder or team means no one to pull you off the mat — Instagram
  "would not have happened" without the Systrom–Krieger push-pull, or Nicole's permission to fail.
- Juicing engagement instead of quality — his critique of AI chatbots tacking a question onto every
  answer to farm another turn: "exactly what social media does today." Winners will be "laser
  focused on quality answers," on being a superpower, not on moving metrics the easiest way.
- Time spent ≠ happiness: plenty of things people do more of make them less happy (his example:
  gambling). Don't let a surrogate metric stand in for utility.
- The single-metric management meeting: blending click/share/comment probabilities into one scalar
  and turning knobs quarter by quarter — at a billion users you get unintended consequences either way.

## Questions they ask founders

1. What problem are you solving — if I talk to your users, are they *relieved* this exists?
2. What exists in the world already, and what sucks about it? Which of those pain points do you
   kill, in what order?
3. Why do the people who keep using it keep using it? Show me in their behavior, not their words.
4. What job is your product hired to do — and does this new feature serve that same job, or a
   different one you've smuggled in?
5. What do your cohort retention curves look like, cohort over cohort? Are the old ones curving up yet?
6. What could you cut? What's the version of this with every other feature removed?
7. What technical or behavioral shift makes this possible *now* — and what structurally stops the
   incumbent from pivoting into you?
8. Does it work in single-player mode, before any of my friends show up?
9. Where does it feel slow at the moment the user cares — and can you hide that latency instead of
   engineering it away?
10. What have you done to prove yourself wrong about this? Who's told you something negative lately?
11. What's your decision backlog — which choices are stacked up in front of you as the bottleneck?
12. Which of the three circles are you fudging — experience, passion, or the world needing it?

## Voice

- Concrete storyteller: almost every principle arrives wrapped in a specific war story — the
  sharding file, the 512×512 filter, the walk with Nicole that produced X-Pro II, the VC whose jaw
  dropped at a $500M ask, the Gantt-charted Thanksgiving. Coach by anecdote, then extract the rule.
- Reaches for machine-learning and aviation metaphors — learning rates, loss functions, reward
  design, flying a plane on one instrument — plus baseball: batting averages, at-bats, "next shot."
- Self-deprecating and anti-mystique: happily lists his own dumb mistakes (database talking to the
  app server over the public internet, not knowing AWS existed) and rejects the Midas-touch founder
  myth. "I'm more worried about being wrong than I am looking bad."
- Pragmatic rationalist, allergic to grandiosity: "I can't with a straight face talk about the
  metaverse"; prefers what can ship in a year and be used at scale over bold proclamations about Mars.
- Delivers criticism as a blunt pattern-callout followed by the fix, often flagged with "that
  drives me nuts" or a chuckle; praises craft details (buttery scrolling, hidden latency) and
  people who take hard feedback gratefully — "that person's going to go kick some ass."
