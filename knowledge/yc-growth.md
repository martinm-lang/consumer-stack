# Y Combinator — Growth & Consumer Playbook

> Sources: _FBivfgOvuE (YC video: How to Get Your First 10 Customers — Max, YC visiting partner), T9ikpoF2GH0 (YC Startup School — Gustaf Alströmer, YC partner, ex-Airbnb growth team), lJausFj_Dto (Dalton & Michael — Michael Seibel & Dalton Caldwell), xFQ5mIJdxhA (The Main Function w/ Garry Tan — guest is Kirsten Green, Forerunner Ventures; consumer-VC perspective adjacent to the YC view)

## Worldview

- **Startup = growth** (Alströmer, citing Paul Graham). Products do not get discovered on their own; "build it and they will come" is not how the world works. If you make something with the potential to be big, making it big is the whole job.
- **Growth work before product-market fit is dangerous** (Alströmer). Applied to a product nobody wants, growth tactics produce a spike that collapses — the fuel evaporates and the curve comes straight down.
- **The first 10 customers never come from a tool** (Max). Customers 1–3 come from your personal network because early buyers are betting on trust in the founder, not product quality. Customers 4–10 come from unscalable manual work. Tools only matter from ~10–20 customers onward.
- **You must hold optimism and pessimism simultaneously** (Seibel/Caldwell). Too pessimistic = you give up after one bad signal. Too optimistic = magical thinking, plans built on "rainbows and ponies." The best founders tolerate the cognitive dissonance of both at once.
- **Intentional growth breaks the natural ceiling** (Alströmer). Facebook's own data science forecast 400M users by 2015; deliberate growth work (translations, mobile shift, internet.org) got them to 2B+. Natural adoption always slows; growth teams exist to push past the forecast.
- **The product is the marketing** (Green). Bad products cannot be marketed into success — consumers are savvy and see through marketing done for its own sake. Stripped of all money and resources, the one thing left to do is nail the product.

## Frameworks

### Where does your buyer actually live? (Max)
Before touching any tool or channel, spend an hour answering about the buyer:
- What does their average day look like? How often do they check email?
- Which conferences do they attend, if any? Are they on Reddit? LinkedIn?
- Do they pick up the phone? Do they buy on friend recommendations? Industry newsletters?
- If you can't answer most of these concretely, you haven't spent enough time with real customers — fix that before anything else.
- Cold email/LinkedIn is the default because it's easy from a laptop, not because it fits the buyer. School-district admins, property managers, insurance agents, truck dispatchers don't live in their inbox.
- One founder closed more in 3 days walking a trade-show floor than in 3 months of cold email to a legacy industry; every hour spent on subject lines had been waste.

### The 1–3 / 4–10 / 10–50 customer ladder (Max)
- **Customers 1–3:** personal network — former colleagues, classmates, friends in the industry, people one intro away. In YC's internal founder survey there were basically no counterexamples. Early buyers are betting on trust in you, so work these in order: first-degree contacts, then second-degree LinkedIn intros, then AI network-search tools (e.g. Happenstance). When asking for an intro, spell out who, why they'd care, and the exact forwardable blurb.
- **Customers 4–10:** things that don't scale — flying out to buyers (one founder flew to the same exec four weeks running until he closed; another got kicked out in Hawaii after eight minutes and later landed the account), Reddit DMs to people complaining about the problem, dinners for six prospects, free consulting sessions, deeply researched personalized outreach. The founder personally doing it signals something no automation can fake: you care enough to spend your own time. That's the one advantage incumbents can't copy.
- **Customers 10–50:** the playbook shifts — refined pitch, case studies, a firm grasp of what resonates. Only now do Apollo (lead database, free tier is enough at first), Clay (AI enrichment/qualification), LinkedIn Premium, and email sequences make sense, because now you have a message worth scaling.

### Product-market fit via retention of a value metric (Alströmer)
1. Pick the one metric that represents the value the customer gets (Airbnb: booked nights; Gusto: payroll runs; Lyft: rides; Checkr: background checks; Stripe: transactions).
2. Decide the ideal natural frequency (Airbnb: annual, because travel cycles are slow; Facebook: daily; Gusto: per pay cycle).
3. Plot cohort retention of that metric over time. Good products flatten out; a curve heading to zero means no PMF and no license to work on growth.
Reference points he gives:
- Shopify: ~10% payment retention at month 1, ~12% at month 12 — ugly top of funnel, but the ones who stay stick forever. PMF; fix onboarding and pour on growth.
- Blue Apron: 50% at month 1, still sliding at month 24 — not an obvious case of PMF.
- Netflix: 70% at 12 months, ~30% at 7 years — definite PMF, spend everything you can on growth.
- Under ~10 users, skip the graphs and just talk to users — ask how they would feel if they could no longer use the product.

### Growth channels by product behavior (Alströmer)
Match the channel to how people already behave; there are very few platforms big enough to build a large company on.
- Rare, high-stakes behavior (buying a house) → people Google it → **SEO / paid search**.
- Users already talk about the product, or more users make it better → **virality & referrals** (engineered word of mouth, optionally with two-sided financial incentives).
- You can literally list every potential customer in a spreadsheet (doctors' offices) → **sales**.
- High LTV and you charge real money → **paid acquisition** — and only then. At scale only four paid channels matter: Facebook, Instagram, Google, YouTube. Free-channel reach is structurally declining; platforms monetize their feeds.
- Don't think about channels at all under ~50 users (arguably under 500).

### Everything is a funnel (Alströmer)
Break any flow — signup, purchase, referral program — into explicit steps, measure each step, optimize each step to the very end.
- Airbnb's referral email was the product of dozens of experiments; nothing in it is random: sender's name in the subject line, one clear value headline (friend sent you $40), a sign-up deadline for urgency, "Accept invitation" instead of "Sign up" for exclusivity, referrer photo + name + tenure for social proof.
- Highest-leverage funnel areas: translation/internationalization, authentication (Airbnb spent years on sign-up and login alone — assume whatever Airbnb/Pinterest show today is the optimized answer), onboarding to first value, purchase conversion.
- First question for any referral program: what percent of weekly active users ever *see* it? Usually a small fraction — you can't use what you can't see.

### Experimentation over gut (Alströmer)
- A shipped feature followed by a metric going up proves nothing — seasonality and news confound everything (soccer app + World Cup; education app + September). Only an A/B counterfactual tells you the truth.
- Without data and experimentation, every decision is either lucky or wrong. At scale you don't really have an option; the alternative cost is a stream of bad decisions made by the loudest voice in the room.
- Airbnb ran experiment review every 2–3 weeks and made the room vote before revealing results; half the room was reliably wrong. Custom full-screen share sheet: +40% shares over native. Renaming a button from "Join trip" to "Accept trip invitation": +14% signups.
- Forecast the best-case outcome before building anything — if best-case is small, don't do it even when the effort is low.
- Test the first slice of a big feature, not all of it; minimize the cost of each experiment and just get better at running them. Out of ideas? Go back to user research and study highly optimized products (Pinterest, Airbnb, Facebook).

### Magical thinking diagnosis (Seibel/Caldwell)
Caldwell's term for founders living in a world of magical causes and effects. The recurring delusions:
- Pitch Michael in an airport and he funds you on the spot — and that's how companies actually get funded.
- The idea/deck has inherent value without code or a single user.
- Money raised, or famous investors, will save you from things not working.
- A marketing or sales hire will solve the biggest pre-PMF problem *for* you — and the world's best marketer would join your pre-MVP startup.
- The trending category guarantees success; the magical break is always one step ahead, zero to 100 in one move.
The tell: every direct question gets a complicated excuse where external parties are to blame and the founder is the hero. The cure is being rooted in actual cause and effect — the way you actually meet Michael is you apply to YC on the website.

### Everything, everywhere, tuned per channel (Green)
The first question every new founder asks is which channel made company X work. The answer:
- You do everything. There is no hack; the mosaic of channels working together is the strategy.
- Define the core, fundamental value you want people to associate with the product, then articulate it deliberately on every surface you can reach.
- No one-size-fits-all message: what resonates via word of mouth, on TikTok, and in an ad are three different messages.
- Product marketing first: what about your product gets better because other people join (network effects)? Those drivers evolve with each tech cycle, but the question is always worth asking.
- Only do marketing that is authentic and additive to the product — consumers see through the rest.
- Prerequisite: something people actually need, or none of the messages mean anything when they land.

### Build into the platform shift, from first principles (Green)
- AI is a platform shift on par with internet and mobile — moving from transactions and attention to relationships and affection. Consumers have never adopted anything this readily.
- Don't take today's product and add AI/memory on top; companies doing that may thrive, but the ones talked about years from now start from a genuinely new place.
- Memory that compounds is the unlock: a continuous learning loop is the new data moat, and retention deepens because the user has *invested* in the relationship — you've moved from transaction to relationship.
- We're in the "messy creative stage": try many interfaces, shoot your shot, iterate. Not everything should be a chat bar — that's an interface designed for ubiquity, not your category. Important topics (health, finances, learning) deserve their own reflective interface.
- Being early buys surprise-and-delight the 30th entrant can't get — but novelty starts a company, never sustains one. Dollar Shave Club's viral video was the step change into early scale, not the business; the business was a demand shift (men entering the personal-care conversation) plus a technology shift (the social web as a new go-to-market).
- Build in the path of where the LLMs are going, never against them. A need plus that tailwind plus a bold new experience gets her attention.

## Heuristics & rules of thumb

- Keep cold outreach under 75 words; longer emails get ignored and read as LLM-written. One clear call to action. Read it aloud to a friend — rewrite anything you wouldn't say to a real person. Follow up 3–4 times over a couple of weeks. (Max)
- Conference playbook: Calendly with back-to-back 15-minute slots all day; email the attendee list twice before and once during the event. Small industry-specific conferences convert far better than big ones. (Max)
- Micro-events: dinner or happy hour for 6–10 ideal-customer-profile people at $50–100/head converts better than large events — nobody ignores your email after eating dinner with you. (Max)
- Frame first outreach as advice/mentorship/feedback rather than a pitch — only if you genuinely mean it. One founder paid lawyers $100–200/hr for feedback; 30% accepted and CAC still penciled out. Give something before you ask: a vulnerability scan, an audit note, specific suggestions — twenty minutes of prep before asking for thirty minutes of someone's time is a fair trade. (Max)
- Find where the pain is complained about in public (Reddit, Facebook groups, Discord, YouTube comments, trade forums) and show up as a real person; DM commenters on old threads one by one. One healthcare founder made 2–5 posts a day answering complaints for months — got shadowbanned from a few subreddits, and got customers. Reddit threads rank on Google for years, so the work compounds. Expect harsh comments and quiet lurker signups. (Max)
- LinkedIn cold motion: send the connection request with no message, then a short DM once they accept — one founder's highest-converting cold channel and the source of his biggest customer. (Max)
- Pre-product pipeline: one founder maxed out weekly LinkedIn connections around one hypothesis per week, talked to 200 salespeople before the product existed — ~50% accepted, 20% of those converted to calls — and had a ready pipeline at launch. (Max)
- Don't buy ads without revenue; CAC must be under LTV with a payback time you can forecast with some accuracy (e.g. $20 CAC on $10/month revenue ≈ two-month payback). Learn attribution before running Google and Facebook simultaneously at scale. (Alströmer)
- Don't hire "a growth person" to own all growth, and don't say "everyone owns growth" — both fail. Split by a clear product line: core product team owns the value moment, growth team owns everything getting people to it. (Alströmer)
- Start A/B testing when you can detect a small-to-medium change (a couple percent) within 2–3 weeks of traffic; below that, don't test at all. Better slightly too early than way too late. (Alströmer)
- Incentive/subsidy programs are scalable if delivered through software and the ROI math proves the users are incremental; hand-delivered coupons are not. (Alströmer)
- Brand marketing is the last thing a startup does — it's unmeasurable; stay in product growth and performance marketing until those hit their limits. Product growth engineers and performance marketers do near-identical data-driven work and should sit together, not on separate floors. (Alströmer)
- Unscalable manual tactics have an expiry date: either stop them when their ROI falls below scalable channels, or turn them into a playbook you rerun city by city. Manually recruiting your first 20 drivers works; against Facebook ads at scale it won't. (Alströmer)
- Self-diagnosis lens: imagine your employees. A pessimistic leader who doesn't believe is off the team immediately; an over-optimistic leader with magical plans loses all credibility in two seconds and everyone starts wondering about payroll. (Seibel/Caldwell)
- SEO: start from keyword research (medium-to-high volume, low competition), not a checklist of tweaks; run your site through an SEO browser — if you only saw the text, would you know what the product does? Ask everyone who writes about you for a link. At scale, SEO is an experimentation discipline (split page groups, vary title tags, measure organic traffic). (Alströmer)
- One negative signal — a bad customer call, a friend's or investor's opinion, a missed demo-day goal — means nothing by itself. Friends and investors who'd never use your product are not predictive. Aggressive goals are for speed; missing one doesn't mean the company is dead. (Seibel/Caldwell)
- 100 un-personalized emails yielding 5 replies is normal, not a verdict. Even when it's working, you mostly get no's. (Seibel/Caldwell)
- Get in before the category is cool. When an idea becomes one of the most common YC applications (chatbots), it's over — the peak of hype is usually right before the collapse. (Seibel/Caldwell)

## Red flags they call out

- Founder pitching a social network with zero code, frustrated that the articulation of the idea isn't treated as valuable — the deck is not the company. (Seibel/Caldwell)
- Believing raised money or famous investors solve problems; turning your brain off after the round. (Seibel/Caldwell)
- Pre-PMF hiring fantasy: "the marketing/sales hire will figure it out" — and the delusion that a world-class person would join your pre-MVP startup anyway. (Seibel/Caldwell)
- Every question answered with a complicated excuse where it's other people's fault; the founder is the hero and external parties are plotting. (Seibel/Caldwell)
- Chasing the trending category after the peak (remote/video startups the second COVID hit — Zoom had years of head start). (Seibel/Caldwell)
- Founders draining the room: if talking about your startup deflates everyone, you're too pessimistic — and pessimists quit, which is the one guaranteed failure. Pessimism, though, is curable with expectation-setting; unrooted optimism is harder. (Seibel/Caldwell)
- Months spent refining cold-email subject lines for a buyer who isn't in their inbox. (Max)
- Weeks setting up prospecting tools while second-degree network intros sit untouched — skipping the lowest-hanging fruit. (Max)
- Growing hard before retention flattens: the spike-and-collapse curve of growth without PMF. (Alströmer)
- "We built the right product, it's just a marketing problem" — if it's a real need and a great product, it gets *some* traction; zero traction means no PMF, not bad marketing. (Green)
- Blanket discount promos (friends-and-family 20% off) as strategy: works only while novel, never for the 30th entrant. Chat-bar-by-default interfaces: designed for ubiquity, not your category. (Green)

## Questions they ask founders

1. Where does your target customer actually spend their day — and can you answer that concretely? (Max)
2. Who are the three people one intro away from you who could be customers this month? (Max)
3. What's the one metric that represents the value a customer gets, and how often should it ideally recur? (Alströmer)
4. What does your retention cohort curve look like — does it flatten, or go to zero? (Alströmer)
5. Can you make a literal spreadsheet of everyone in the world who would use this? Then why aren't you doing sales? (Alströmer)
6. What percent of your active users have ever *seen* your referral program? (Alströmer)
7. What's your evidence this works — an experiment, or the loudest voice in the room? (Alströmer)
8. Walk me through the actual cause and effect of your plan — which step is magic? (Seibel/Caldwell)
9. Why do they need your product, why will they come back, and why will they add another thing to their already crowded lives? (Green)
10. What would this experience look like built from first principles on the new platform, instead of last generation's interface plus AI? (Green)
