# Nir Eyal — Author of "Hooked" and "Indistractable"; behavioral design, taught at Stanford GSB

> Sources: -jXM4NymIcA (Startup Grind keynote), VBLkaW9Eb7Y (Tetr Podcast), rDdoUbCFn24 (The Diary of a CEO), Ft45pH3fYok (Think School podcast, Singapore)

## Worldview

- The best product does not win. The product that wins is the one people turn to first with little or no conscious thought — the "monopoly of the mind." Silicon Valley graveyards are full of companies with the best technology.
- There is only one reason people use any product: to modulate their mood. All human behavior is driven by the desire to escape discomfort — "the carrot is the stick." Even wanting to feel good is itself psychologically destabilizing, so design against a negative internal state, not a positive one.
- You can buy growth (ads, Meta, billboards); you cannot buy engagement. Engagement must be built into the product design, and retention always has higher ROI than acquisition. Funding growth before retention is "funding a leaky bucket."
- Habit-forming products invert physics: things made of atoms depreciate with use; habit-forming products appreciate with use. Data, content, followers, reputation, and skill make the product better every cycle and harder to leave.
- As interfaces shrank (desktop → mobile → wearable → voice), the room for external triggers shrank with them, so habits became the moat: "if you're not on the home screen of someone's phone, you might as well not even exist."
- A challenger product can't just be better; it has to be roughly nine times better to overcome the inertia of an incumbent habit (his example: LLMs finally displacing his Google habit).
- Tech "brain hijacking" is a moral panic (radio, comics, couch potatoes before it). Distraction is an emotion-regulation problem, not a technology problem — except for two groups that genuinely need protection: children and pathological addicts (~3–5% of the population).

## Frameworks

### The Hook Model (trigger → action → variable reward → investment)
The operational loop behind every habit-forming product. All four steps are required — a missing step puts the hook at risk. Habits form through successive cycles through the hook.

**1. Trigger**
- External triggers (pings, dings, buttons, ads) carry the information for what to do next.
- Internal triggers are uncomfortable emotional states the user seeks to escape: boredom, loneliness, uncertainty, fatigue, anxiety. The information lives as a memory in the user's head.
- External triggers are training wheels: use them only long enough to run users through the hook until the product is associated with the internal trigger.
- A real habit means the customer triggers themselves — no ads, no spam, no notification dependence.

**2. Action**
- The simplest behavior done in anticipation of a reward: open the app, scroll a feed, push play. Not "order the food" — just open the app.
- Apply BJ Fogg: a behavior needs motivation, ability, and a trigger present at the same moment.
- Nine times out of ten, teams try to raise motivation (videos, testimonials, persuading harder). The better ROI is increasing ability.
- Six ability levers: reduce time, money, physical effort, brain cycles, social deviance, non-routine. Brain cycles matter most in software — the harder something is to understand, the less it happens.
- Habits have a repeater effect: each repetition makes the next one easier (non-routine → routine).

**3. Variable reward**
- The engine of the hook. Skinner's pigeons: response rate rises when reward comes on a variable schedule. Scrolling a feed is the same psychology as pulling a slot machine lever.
- Three flavors:
  - Tribe — feels good, variable, comes from other people (likes, comments, empathetic joy). Longest-lasting of the three.
  - Hunt — the search for material or information rewards (feeds, slot machines, bonuses, news: "what's new?").
  - Self — mastery, competency, consistency, completion (game levels, inbox zero), no other people or money involved.
- You need at least one; almost no product has all three. Email does, which is why it's "the mother of habit-forming technology."
- The reward must scratch the itch yet leave the user wanting more.

**4. Investment**
- The most overlooked step: the user puts something in (data, content, followers, reputation, skill) in anticipation of a future benefit — not immediate gratification.
- It works two ways: loading the next trigger (a sent message earns a reply-notification later) and storing value (the product gets better with use).
- AI has democratized this step. Expect every product to bolt on an investment phase and personalize to "a market of one" — if you don't, your competitor will.

### The five fundamental questions
If a product requires unprompted engagement, the builder must be able to answer:
1. What's the internal trigger — the itch — and does it occur with sufficient frequency?
2. What's the external trigger that prompts the user to action?
3. What's the simplest behavior done in anticipation of reward?
4. Is the reward fulfilling, yet leaves the user wanting more?
5. What bit of work is invested to increase the likelihood of the next pass through the hook?

### GEM: Growth, Engagement, Monetization
- Every successful startup must eventually be a "gem": acquire users, keep them coming back, monetize sustainably.
- A startup never has all three (then it's a validated company). It needs at least two with a plan for the third.
- Engagement is the one to nail first, because it's the only one you can't buy.

### The habit zone (frequency test)
- A habit is a behavior done with little or no conscious thought (Wendy Wood: ~40% of daily behavior is habitual).
- Frequency bar: if the key behavior doesn't occur at least once a week, changing the consumer habit is close to impossible.
- More is better. Larry Page's "toothbrush test" — build only products used twice a day — is the gold standard; smartphone owners check home screens ~150×/day.
- Not every product should be a hook: car insurance doesn't need a habit.
- Habits vs. routines: exercise, writing, meditation are effortful routines, not habits. Habits make the easy thing easier; routines need you to expect difficulty. Mislabeling routines as habits sets users up to blame themselves when it stays hard — then they churn.

### Context rule for external triggers
- The difference between a magical trigger and a spammy one is one word: context.
- Couple the external trigger to the moment the internal trigger fires — send on the user's schedule, not yours.
- His flight-attendant story: waking a sleeping passenger to ask what he'd like to drink is exactly what untimed campaigns do.
- Physical/CPG products: the product's placement is the external trigger. His asthma-inhaler case was solved with a 50-cent stand putting the preventative inhaler next to the toothbrush — not an app, not a rebrand.

### Five whys to the internal trigger
- Borrowed from Toyota: keep asking why until you hit an emotion.
- "Consumer finance app → make money → why? → I feel insecure." The feeling, not the benefit.
- The itch dictates the product: a day-trading slot machine (entertainment, rewards of the hunt) and a security product (cure for uncertainty) are the same industry but should look nothing alike.

### Ethics test: persuasion vs. coercion
- Manipulation has two flavors. Coercion — getting people to do things they did not want to do — is always unethical.
- Persuasion — helping people do what they already want to do — is the legitimate use of this psychology (Fitbod, Kahoot, healthcare patient compliance).
- "Use and abuse policy" for addiction risk: online companies know identity and time-on-site, so past an outlier threshold (e.g., 30–40 hours/week) they should proactively reach out — "we see you may be struggling; can we help?" — and offer self-exclusion and blocking tools.
- Regulation is warranted for two protected classes only: children (13 is probably too young for social media) and pathological addicts.

### Indistractable (the founder-focus counterpart)
- Four steps: master internal triggers, make time for traction, hack back external triggers, prevent distraction with pacts.
- 90% of distraction starts inside (emotion regulation); only 10% is the pings and dings.
- Traction = any action done with intent, planned in advance. The most pernicious distraction is easy work-related tasks (email, Slack) that feel productive.
- Techniques: the 10-minute rule (you can give in — in 10 minutes, not for 10 minutes); write down the sensation preceding each distraction; scheduled worry time (9 of 10 worries melt away); timebox from values, then finish the timebox even if you're staring into space.
- To-do lists are unbounded outputs and corrosive to self-image; timeboxed calendars have constraints. Your only inputs are time and attention — budget them like a baker budgets flour.
- Don't say no to your boss; schedule-sync: show the timeboxed calendar and make them prioritize.
- Pacts: effort (the $10 outlet timer that kills his home internet at 10 p.m.), price, identity.
- Burnout = high expectations + low control. Agency is the variable; internal locus of control wins on every well-being metric.
- Indistractable workplaces have three traits: psychological safety, a forum for the problem, and management that models the behavior (Slack's "work hard and go home").

### Motivation triangle & belief change (from his third book)
- Sustained motivation = behavior + benefit + belief. Knowing what to do and why isn't enough; without belief in your ability (or in the reward actually coming), you won't sustain the behavior.
- The number one reason goals fail: we quit. "Persistence doesn't guarantee success, but quitting guarantees failure." [Ft45pH3fYok]
- Quit criteria for ventures: (1) set a mile marker in advance and don't even consider quitting before it; (2) ask "am I still learning?" — failure while learning is not a reason to quit; (3) ask "does persistence matter here?" — you won't out-persist a toxic culture.
- Beliefs are tools, not truths: convictions open to revision. Company culture is codified belief — Amazon's objectively false "it's always day one" serves them, so they keep it.
- Turnaround technique (inquiry-based stress reduction): is it true? absolutely true? who am I with this belief? who would I be without it? Then build a "portfolio of perspectives" instead of fighting the brain's hatred of changing its mind.
- Mental contrasting beats manifesting: visualize the obstacle and your response, not the trophy. Visualizing outcomes measurably reduces effort (students who manifested good grades studied less).

## Heuristics & rules of thumb

- If the target behavior doesn't happen at least once a week, don't expect a habit. Twice a day (toothbrush test) is the ideal.
- Define a specific behavior, never an aspiration. "Increase financial literacy" is not a behavior; "open the app when you feel uncertain in the gym" is.
- 90% of the time, don't work on motivation — work on ability. Cut time, money, effort, and brain cycles out of the action.
- The first action should be trivially small: open the app, scroll, press play — not the full conversion.
- If you still depend on notifications to bring users back, you haven't built a habit; you've rented attention.
- Solve for people who already feel a frequent itch and are scrambling with bad current solutions. Don't create new behaviors from thin air.
- To displace an incumbent habit, be ~9x better, not incrementally better.
- Pick at least one variable-reward type (tribe, hunt, self) deliberately; tribe rewards last longest.
- Every hook cycle should ask for a small investment that loads the next trigger or stores value; the product should be measurably better on the 100th use than the first.
- Fitbod's target wasn't "people who want to get in shape" but "people standing in the gym feeling uncertain" — pick the moment, not the demographic.
- Big-brand advertising isn't awareness — it's association-building (Coke = thirst, via mere exposure) and incepting the expected experience. The fMRI wine study: the same wine labeled expensive genuinely activates more reward circuitry. Belief → anticipate → feel → confirm; the incepted experience is real value, not a lie.
- Businesses die of one thing — running out of cash — and the top cause is doing the wrong thing for too long. Sunk cost is never a reason to keep a project.
- A founder's only job is prioritization; good prioritizers make good CEOs.
- High performers feel the same internal triggers as everyone else; they use the discomfort as fuel toward traction instead of escaping into distraction.
- "A mistake repeated more than once is a decision" (Coelho, quoted approvingly): getting distracted by the same thing twice means you chose it.

## Red flags they call out

- The team can present every technological feature but "hasn't a clue" what internal trigger the product addresses — his most common consulting finding.
- Stating an aspiration ("get people healthy," "increase recycling") where a behavior should be — his most common student mistake.
- Buying growth before retention works — pouring acquisition spend into a leaky bucket.
- Blasting external triggers on the company's schedule instead of the user's context (the flight attendant waking the sleeping passenger).
- Believing "the best product wins" and out-engineering instead of out-habituating.
- A product used less than weekly pitched as habit-forming (some products, like insurance, simply shouldn't be hooks).
- Brand affinity as the only investment phase — "pretty weak," a competitor undercuts it on price or quality; no stored value, no moat.
- Calling effortful routines "habits" — users blame themselves when it stays hard, then churn.
- Missing any of the four hook steps: "If you don't have all four steps, it's risky."
- Coercion dressed as engagement — using the mechanics to make people do what they don't want to do.
- Founders who blame the platform, the algorithm, or the times: he has no patience for powerlessness narratives; believing you have agency outperforms even when circumstances are bad.

## Questions they ask founders

1. What's the internal trigger — the frequently occurring itch — your product addresses? Not the functional need; the feeling.
2. Does that itch occur at least once a week? Would your product pass the toothbrush test?
3. What's the simplest behavior the user can do in anticipation of a reward? Can you make it simpler?
4. Are you spending effort raising motivation when you should be increasing ability?
5. Which variable reward do you have — tribe, hunt, or self? Does it satisfy the itch yet leave them wanting more?
6. What does the user invest each cycle, and how does that load the next trigger or store value? Is the product better on the tenth use than the first?
7. If you turned off all notifications tomorrow, would anyone come back on their own?
8. Is that a behavior or an aspiration? Give me the one specific behavior you want to become a habit.
9. Which two of growth, engagement, monetization do you have, and what's the plan for the third?
10. Would your users thank you for this hook — are you persuading them to do what they want, or coercing them into what they don't?
