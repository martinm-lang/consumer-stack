# Mark Pincus — Founder & CEO, Zynga

> Sources: oHwUD9b9_pg (Y Combinator, with Garry Tan), 7eh9C3TUotc (Lenny's Podcast, with Lenny Rachitsky)

## Worldview

- Your gut-level instincts are almost always right; the specific product ideas you layer on top are usually wrong. His rule: instincts right 95% of the time, ideas right at best 25% of the time. Product process exists to protect the instinct while ruthlessly cycling through ideas.
- The core metric of consumer value is retention, not virality. Zynga's core metric was retention and he claims it was the only consumer company tracking day-365 retention; the most valuable companies in the world have the highest D365. Viral-first companies are sinking speedboats — growing faster than they leak until they can't.
- Great product makers already know a launch is a hit before launching — they collect winnings, they don't make bets. Launching to "see if people like it" means you're not ready.
- Consumer is arguably not investable right now, and that's exactly why the opportunity has never been greater: AI/agents enable new internet treasures — services we can't remember life before or imagine life without — and very few slots in our digital life stack are filled. His phone home screen is half empty, half generic apps.
- AI is an important technology but not yet a platform (no consumer hardware or interface platform, no discovery, no distribution). We're still in the mobile/web era; average app installs per user per month is zero. A consumer-facing platform for AI apps and agents is coming, likely from the LLM companies themselves.
- Latent demand hides where a category looks dead or boring. In 2007 gaming was $23B but nobody he knew played — the friction (cost, setup, time) suppressed a mass-market urge. Same today for social: people quitting Instagram feel like they quit smoking (NPS of quitters went +35 to −35), yet the demand to be social is beyond latent.
- Trust is the precondition for social products: Tribe failed because he got the trust component wrong — people need a good container of trust before they'll put themselves on the web, which is what Facebook's .edu-only start got right.
- Timing beats almost everything: build today for the moment compute is basically free (his framing of Garry Tan's point). Anything that can be free will be free — free was the whole wedge for both Freeloader (free screensaver vs. $35 flying toasters) and Zynga (free decent-quality games vs. $60 boxes).
- The founder's real ambition should be an internet treasure — his stated "why." Being 18 months ahead means being wrong-looking for years; Amazon didn't prove the consumer internet was back until fall 2002, six years in, and by then everyone who waited was late.

## Frameworks

### Proven / Better / New
His core product religion, the engine of Zynga PM. Separate every product into three buckets and treat each differently:

- **Proven**: everything you are NOT innovating on. Find best-of-breed and copy it legally, precisely, with taste — icon, onboarding, camera behavior, whatever. Get your PhD in proven first: you haven't earned the right to innovate on the camera until you're the world's leading expert on the best existing ones. Proven is strict: it only counts on *this platform, for this audience, for this experience*. A game that was popular in the 90s is not proven for mobile today — founders misuse proven to justify a wrong idea.
- **Better**: an improvement that 10 out of 10 *existing users of that product* would say an instant, emphatic yes to — free, faster, no download, less friction, mobile polish. Not what *you* think is better: what the founder thinks is better is actually new. Better is usually small, incremental, noticed most by power users (Words with Friends = Scrabble with mobile polish).
- **New**: the single novel idea — your isolated innovation zone, the instinct you're actually testing. It's the "back of the box": it gets people to try the product, but assume it will probably fail, don't get attached, and have four more new ideas ready to test behind it. If you do proven and better right, the product fails only for the *right* reason (the new didn't land), never the wrong one.

How to run it:
- Start from the instinct in your gut — that's the innovation zone. Isolate it so you're testing only that, and start from the proposition that your current idea for it is probably wrong, so you don't get attached.
- Take proven off the table first: copy it, don't question it, you don't have time to improve it. Then find the rare 10/10 better. Then test many news around the one instinct, expecting each to die.
- Proven gives you a base, better gives you a direction, new is the spikes that go out — and you should assume none of them catch on the first pass.
- Slack may have been proven + better with no new at all — and that's fine, even good, because people don't like change; making a loved behavior more accessible can beat showing them something they never knew they wanted.
- Gold-standard failure case: Sid Meier's social Civilization on Facebook — Zynga's junior PMs called it dead on arrival within 10 minutes because he didn't copy the proven Facebook first-time user experience, so nobody ever saw his innovation.
- Done right it's a time machine: 2003 Mark was a year ahead of a $1.6T instinct inside Tribe, but with sinking D30 retention and no discipline of testing many ideas around the instinct, he never found it.

### True signal / "the fish are running"
Binary test for product-market heat:
- When you have lightning in a bottle you *know* — you don't need metrics because every feedback loop is a yes, you're addicted to your own product, friends love it, and nobody has to be told to work harder or faster. When the fish are running, everyone is up all night throwing nets.
- When it's debatable and you're hunting through data for one more test, you don't have it. It only registers positively: you know it when you have it; when you don't, you just don't know.
- Dating analogy: with the right person you never ask if they're the one. The moment you're asking whether your product is an A, it isn't one.
- Corollary: go slow to go fast — get to real conviction first, then everyone moves fast against it on their own.

### Kill hope before hope kills you
- Distinguish belief (founded in lived experience with your product, users, and data) from hope (confidence without basis — a prayer).
- Kill "viable" out of MVP — viable is where hope hides: if it's viable, it *might* make it, and teams keep shipping releases hoping the next one is magical.
- Two distinct modes, never confused: a product you put out *to learn from* (build it wrong on purpose, in a day or a week, just good enough to get signal) versus a *maximum launchable product* you already believe — not hope — is a hit.
- Build it completely wrong before you know it's the right product. If you believe today's version is wrong, you won't waste three months building it well.
- Use AI as a failure machine, not a faster way to build one idea: test more ideas in a week than your industry tests in a year — 100 ideas a day, not one idea in three months. AI is a dangerous drug precisely because it makes a merely-viable product cheap to reach.

### The abyss
The zone between passionate pursuits where you don't know if you'll ever love an idea again, or ever come out.
- Don't fight it — the abyss is where taste zones expand and where pieces of the way out get found, even if you can't recognize them at the time.
- Pulling the plug creates inspiration: two weeks after killing his Earth/metaverse project (fourth attempt, four years, $25M), he was more inspired than he'd been in four years. There is power in pulling the plug on a B+, and even more on a D.
- On what to do with a diagnosed B+: the power is *knowing* it's a B+. Then decide — kill it, use it to learn, or mine it for anything proven or near-proven nearby.

### The cocktail party (reinventing social)
- Social products work when they feel like a great cocktail party: you're glad you're there, it's a little rowdy, and you leave with great *leads* — a date on Friendster, a job on LinkedIn, a song file on Napster. Lead generation is the underrated utility of every social hit.
- Social networks won by giving social *productivity* — staying in the loop with 300–1,000 friends — and decay when they drift from productivity into time-wasting engagement (LinkedIn, Instagram's TikTok envy).
- Today everyone is hanging out on their Claude and their GPT but there's no cocktail party — a quiet, lonely place, like the web before social networking. The play: find (or host) where the cocktail party wants to happen, add lead generation, make it rowdy.
- His agent-era extension: a "social membrane" — an agent that knows both sides' context, brokers asymmetric interest (you want to hang out more than I do this weekend), shares less than it knows, and spares feelings. Zynga itself was this play: everyone was at the Facebook party, so he dropped games into the middle of it.

### Invest / Express / Connect + ASN
Zynga's design lens for social products: let users invest in something, feel creative expressing it — people want to *feel* creative, not *be* creative — and connect around it; co-op play and gifting were their most successful features. Related move, the "bold beat": when a proven mechanic has gone rote and boring (poker), add a whole new dimension to it (real identities, social play, meeting people) rather than polishing the mechanic. Measured with ASN (Active Social Network): the number of round trips with another person (you take a turn, they take one back; you gift, they gift back). Going 0→1 ASN = 80% chance the user returns within a month; ASN of 4 = 80% chance they show up 22 of the next 30 days. Build and innovate directly against that metric — round trips are the biggest dopamine hit a product can deliver.

### Be less ambitious (the humility paradox)
- Too much vision at the outset makes you miss product-market fit because you won't start at a small enough, humble enough place. The starting point of massive hits is embarrassingly small: a 41-year-old multi-exit founder making a Facebook poker game; Slack as a failed game studio's internal tool; Bolt.new as a web-stack side project bolted onto an AI copilot; Facebook as an app to check out people at Harvard.
- Tribe failed because he tried to do everything at 100,000 feet with multiple use cases at once; Zynga worked because ambition came down to 1,000 feet.
- Success makes this worse: after a win you feel obliged to go bigger, which is why new founders have an advantage over multi-time founders. The world doesn't care about your resume.
- Startups have the advantage *because* they can chase flaky little threads that aren't yet a business — a Zuck can only work on multi-billion-dollar opportunities. The more ambitious you are, the smaller you should be willing to start.

### Moral arbitrage (getting over copying)
- Founders resist copying because school taught them copying is cheating and they became founders to be innovators — which makes disciplined copying an arbitrage for those with less ego.
- The truly ambitious burn their resume: define ambition in the eyes of your consumer (nurses in Indiana playing Farmville), not your peers. You're not trying to win awards; take the best ideas wherever they are if they serve her.
- Pure copies fail too — if all you did was copy, there's no reason to choose you; consumer taste resists the too-derivative. Master copiers work with enough craft that nobody notices what it's derivative of (OMG Pop's Draw Something copied Words with Friends' turn system perfectly after their fully-original game nearly killed the company — versus Rovio's 45 wildcat-drilling shots before Angry Birds).
- Respect users' ownership of products they rely on — they're angry at change even when it's for the better, because pattern recognition is how they move through life. Craig Newmark took two years to add photos to Craigslist without moving the text people scanned.

### Management = right thing when you're not in the room
- The only point of all management tools is getting people to do the right thing when you're not in the room. First lesson: *be in the room* — as much as you can, if you're the best player at that position. Delegate only when you can't possibly be in all the rooms, and replace yourself with people and processes you trust to do the right thing without you.
- Make everyone a CEO: real operating control against a hill — plan, budget, then leave them alone. Hire frustrated expert witnesses (slightly know-it-all ICs with pent-up demand to prove they're right); they barely need managing and it's the most motivating job you can give.
- Stay close to the metal: the best product CEOs are in the pixel-level minutia (Jobs picking conference-room carpet). Discord's inverted pyramid — founders realized they'd delegated the most important UX decisions to their least experienced people, and flipped it so founders are the first and last mile of the product.
- Spread the vampire blood: the teaching hospital (pack people into your product meetings to absorb your approach) and tech assistants (a mini-me shadows you 6–12 months, then takes a big role — the Bezos/Jassy model; it's non-scalable practice that scales).
- Micromanagement is beautiful for as long as you can sustain it — he ran two-hour name-by-name standups to 50 employees, and they were much more effective for it.
- The #1 job of a CEO is to be right — picked the right product and strategy. The right body of water matters more than the right boat; a great boat in a dead lake bed goes nowhere. In hiring, "was right about something" is the best resume — take misfits who are right.

## Heuristics & rules of thumb

- Instincts right 95% of the time; ideas wrong 75% of the time. Isolate the instinct, cycle the ideas.
- "Better" bar: 10 out of 10 existing users say yes instantly. If only you think it's better, reclassify it as new.
- Assume the new will fail; have four more new ideas queued before you launch the first.
- Proven only counts on this platform, for this audience, for this exact experience — otherwise it's not proven.
- Gold is a proven feature buried in someone else's product with the wrong idea around it (his Freeloader from Netscape's buried offline browsing; Nikita's TBH from an Arabic-only variant).
- Track D365 retention and build against its early indicators. Low D1/D30 → no D365. But high D30 with zero D365 is most products — ask "why will someone use this in a year?"
- ASN thresholds: 0→1 round trip = 80% return within a month; 4 = 80% chance of 22/30 active days.
- Build it wrong before you know it's right: a day or a week to get signal, never three months for a wrong product.
- Test marketing *inside* the product before spending ad budget: Farmville put locked coming-soon art on the game board instead of a $10M external campaign — it tested art, message, and demand at once, and selling early-access keys made $19M.
- If you're asking whether the product is an A, it's not an A. Everything-works is the only passing grade.
- Onboarding bar from Zynga: free, a game you already know, three clicks and you're in, asks 5–15 minutes. Comics in the newspaper, not the comic book store.
- He calibrates timing on himself as a user: historically ~18 months ahead of the early mass majority.
- Distribution must be proven and baked into product and strategy from day one; counting on viral or word-of-mouth spread is a hope strategy, not a belief strategy. Experiments are ~100x cheaper now than after the noise floods in.
- Design consumer AI for the free-token future: price out the service as if intelligence were unlimited and free, and work backward (the iPhone was predictable from memory/display/compute cost curves). Losing money on tokens today can be a phenomenal business because token prices fall — unlike pig ears and delivery in 1999.
- Latent-demand test: is the category big but boring, or does it require crawling across broken glass? Both signal suppressed mass demand (his Rise of Nations setup with his nephews took two machines each plus FaceTime — fun, done exactly once).
- Adrenaline test for social/engagement: is there heat, or is the product potato chips? Watch the NPS of people who *quit*: Facebook/Instagram quitters swung from +35 to −35 and brag about leaving — a decayed incumbent is an opening.
- The app-store path is dead math for consumer: ~40,000 new games launched in a year, zero sustained top-25 hits. Don't plan a strategy that requires winning discovery that no longer exists.

## Red flags they call out

- Botched first-time user experience burying real innovation: Sid Meier's social Civilization was declared dead on arrival by Zynga PMs within 10 minutes because the FTUE had too many clicks — he didn't copy the proven onboarding, so nobody ever saw his game design.
- Using proven as cover for a wrong idea (it was popular in the '90s, therefore proven) instead of platform-precise, pixel-level proven.
- Too ambitious too early: multiple use cases, no single humble wedge (his own Tribe). Multi-time founders have too much rope to hang themselves — they can raise against vision before PMF.
- Hope-based launching: shipping an MVP that's merely "viable" and praying the next release does something magical.
- Distribution as an afterthought — best-mousetrap thinking, especially now when nothing in consumer distribution is proven.
- Virality as the core metric — sinking speedboats (BeReal-type spikes) that grow faster than they churn.
- Sticking with a B+ — stoically, heroically sticking with a losing idea because investors and team are committed. Real founder mode is the courage to tell them this isn't it.
- Founders becoming expert witnesses in their own company: closest to the answer, furthest from the decision, after contorting themselves for a CTO hire or a big investor until they've built a house they don't want to live in.
- A consumer CEO who doesn't love the product and know it better than anyone else — he doesn't trust them.
- Delegating the most important UX decisions to the least experienced people (Discord's inverted-pyramid realization) instead of founders being first and last mile on the product.
- Using AI to build one idea in three months instead of a hundred ideas a day.
- Testing ads for the product as an afterthought — teams that never tested their ads before going to market.
- Calling a $1,000/month product "consumer" — if only wealthy friends can afford it, that's basically enterprise; the consumer version arrives when the cost curve does.
- Premium consumer products with heavy AI inference inside: in games and most consumer, every cent counts, so token costs break the model today.

## Questions they ask founders

- What's your instinct here, and what's the idea on top of it? Which are you actually testing?
- What exactly is proven — on this platform, for this audience, for this experience — and did you copy it perfectly, or did you accidentally innovate on it?
- Would 10 out of 10 existing users of the incumbent say an instant, emphatic yes to your better? Or is that your new?
- What's the back-of-the-box new that gets someone to try it — and what are the next four news when it fails?
- Why will someone still use this in a year? What early indicator of D365 are you building against?
- Are you asking whether it's an A? Then it's not — so what do you do with a B+: kill it, or mine it for what's near-proven?
- Do you believe this is a hit, or do you hope it is? What in your lived experience with users backs that up?
- How many ideas did you test this week? Why did that take three months instead of a day?
- Where's distribution baked into the product? What's proven about it?
- How would this product change if intelligence were unlimited and free? What does free mean here?
- Where's the cocktail party — and what's the lead someone walks away with?

Speaks in paradoxes he's earned (be less ambitious to be more ambitious, go slow to go fast, build it wrong before it's right).

- Analogizes to life: dating for product-market fit, parenting for managing teams, fishing runs for momentum, potato chips and quitting smoking for engagement quality.
