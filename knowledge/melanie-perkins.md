# Melanie Perkins — Co-founder & CEO, Canva

> Sources: -LywX3T5Scc (Lenny's Podcast), EbiGblxQmIE (SXSW Sydney keynote), QIRLeqKxy8c (Goldman Sachs Private Company Conference)
> Note: a fourth planned source (nyc0UiuUfPo, New Economies) turned out to feature co-founder Cameron Adams, not Perkins, and was used for his playbook instead.

## Worldview

- Plan from the future backwards, not from today's constraints forwards. Most planning is "looking at the bricks around you and stacking them"; she starts from the magical future she wants to exist, then builds a ladder of tiny steps back to today. For Canva, planning from her actual situation (student, no software experience) "would have been nothing frankly" — the whole company came from the improbable end.
- The core product promise is friction removal: take a person's idea and turn it into a design with as little friction as possible between those two points. Every big bet (templates, drag-and-drop, AI) is judged as an extension of that one premise.
- Product has exactly two parts: building toward the mission, and listening to the community and building what they ask for. Both run permanently and in parallel.
- Canva deliberately sits "smack bang in the middle" of the creativity/productivity Venn diagram — refusing to make customers choose between the two suites is the strategic position.
- Software historically made people feel dumb; people spent their lives believing they weren't creative. The product's job is to make people feel empowered and confident within minutes — she treats emotional confidence as a design requirement, not a nice-to-have.
- Getting rich is not a goal, it's a means to an end. The two-step plan (build one of the world's most valuable companies; do the most good we can do) is operational, not decorative — and step two fuels step one (education users become workplace users; mission-driven people join and stay).

## Frameworks

### Column A vs Column B planning
Column A: look at the resources you have and ask what you can stack out of them. Column B: imagine the perfect future ("what would publishing look like in the future?"), commit to it even though it's completely improbable, then work backwards. Application: write down the future state first — she keeps a physical wall in her office with her vision of the world in 2050 — then find the first rung of the ladder, however microscopic. The two ends feel embarrassingly mismatched (giant dream, tiny step); do it anyway, because the steps compound over a decade.

### Chaos to clarity
Every idea starts at maximum chaos (an idea, a problem, a belief in your head) and must be walked step by step toward clarity. Each step adds a little clarity: write it down → make a pitch deck → turn it into designs → turn it into a prototype → real. The very first step is the embarrassing one because you have no mastery and none of the answers. The mechanism matters because ideas that stay in your head cannot be willed into existence — nobody else can see them. Every Canva project kicks off with a vision deck for exactly this reason.

### Mission → mission pillars → crazy big goals
The mission ("empower the world to design") is decomposed into literal pillars: everyone / anything / every ingredient / every language / every device. Each pillar gets successive yearly goals that chip away at it (English → Spanish → 20 languages → 100 → right-to-left languages → true localization). The test of the system: what people actually spend their time on ladders directly up to the mission on the wall. She calls the mission-on-a-wall-but-work-goes-elsewhere pattern the common failure, and says the magic — and customer authenticity — comes from closing that gap.

### Crazy big goals + celebrations
Goals should be big enough that "you feel completely inadequate before it" — that inadequacy is the point, because it forces you to work to will it into existence. Reasonable goals get abandoned at the first roadblock. Two riders: (1) the goal must be something you genuinely want, because willing it into existence takes years; (2) compress achievement into a moment in time and celebrate it hard (smashed Greek plates, released doves) so a decade-long climb has rungs worth marking. Timing accuracy is explicitly sacrificed: "we have not been very accurate with timing" — 6-month estimates became 2 years, and that's acceptable if direction holds.

### Closing the loop
More than a million community requests a year get tallied, broken down, and delivered to product teams; when a request ships, Canva emails the requester to tell them. Over 200 loops closed in a year, from gradient text to an entire sheets product. People are "completely shocked" that a company actually listened — she considers that shock a competitive advantage and finds it bizarre that this isn't the status quo. The same machinery transferred directly to enterprise: enterprises have loops too, and the 101-workplace-features launch came straight from enterprise complaint loops.

### Rejection as pitch-refinement fuel
100+ investor rejections, each converted into a deck edit: "market's not big enough" → new slide sizing the market; "you're the same as X" → new slide mapping every player and the gap; investors don't know the industry → open with the problem and lay of the land. Iterate until every objection is pre-answered and a 6-hour conversation compresses into a short deck. Corollary: lead with the problem — "if people don't understand the problem then they can't understand or care about your solution." Some people will never get it (the Lean Startup investor was never going to fund the anti-lean startup); find your tribe instead of converting everyone.

### Cupcake and icing
The core product (cupcake) stays identical for every segment — student, small business, enterprise. Each segment gets only a thin icing layer (e.g. IT admin for enterprise). This is why kid-driven feedback improves the enterprise product and why serving new segments doesn't fork the platform.

### Bottom-up freemium engine
Deep investment in a genuinely valuable free product → people confidently share it → Pro → Teams → Enterprise, in that order, waiting years before formally catering to enterprise. Virality is engineered, not hoped for: certificates at 5/10/100 designs, playful launch moments (the "glow up portal"), fun as workplace differentiator (confetti on the C key). ~90% of growth organic.

## Heuristics & rules of thumb

- 10 random people from the internet give feedback representative of millions. She has personally run hundreds, possibly thousands, of user tests and treats hesitation over a button as a real defect signal.
- Run user tests online, not in the room — people alone with their camera are far more frank.
- Get users to a confidence-building win within about 5 minutes of first touch (the "put a hat on the monkey" starter challenges existed because testers were literally scared to touch buttons).
- Better to solve a small number of people's problem really well than a large number of people's problem badly — but check the pain maps to a larger market before committing.
- Ignore competitors; hunt for the gap in the market where a real pain point exists and people will pay because it's truly solved. Fusion Books → Canva came from a customer asking "can I use this for newsletters?", not from wedge strategy.
- Expect every system to break each time the company doubles; rebuild it in a way authentic to your company rather than importing another company's process ("taking someone else's bricks and sticking them in your house" doesn't match).
- A value is only as good as what you'd spend significant money to uphold — anyone can write words on a wall.
- Adopt AI (or any hot technology) only where it genuinely helps customers achieve their goals; investor enthusiasm is not a reason to put it front and center. Name features for the customer outcome, not the tech ("we call it Magic Studio because we want it to feel like magic").
- Fundraising: convert rejection into things you control — the deck and the number of people you talk to. Talk to literally everyone.
- Run the "wild success / terrible failure in 10 years" exercise for the company and for each area of it.
- Take the 1% pledge (time, product, profit, equity) early — trivial to do at the start, compounds enormously.
- Protect perspective structurally: no email or Slack on the phone; all-in when working, all-out when not. Working every weekend can mean missing that you're working on the wrong thing.

## Red flags they call out

- A solution looking for a problem — and pitches that jump to the cool solution before anyone understands or cares about the problem.
- Mission-on-the-wall companies: what the org actually does and how it makes money point in a different direction from the stated mission.
- Copying process off the shelf from bigger companies — she says Canva tried this "so many times over" and it repeatedly failed versus doing what was authentic to them.
- Reasonable, realistic goals — they get dropped the moment problems arrive ("okay, I won't bother with that").
- AI front and center because it's the rage rather than because it serves the customer's goal.
- Never making time to imagine; being permanently dragged into the inbox/Slack present, only stacking today's bricks.
- Doing exactly what everyone else in the ecosystem does because you're marinating in the same buzz — she credits being in Australia, one step removed, with the freedom to "just go do what feels right."
- Product companies not shipping (she lived it for two years and calls it the least fun thing they ever did — tolerable only because the rewrite was existential for cross-platform, collaboration, and team scaling).

## Questions they ask founders

1. What is wild success for this in 10 years? And what is terrible failure?
2. What future do you actually want to exist — and is this product a rung on the ladder toward it, or just the bricks you happened to have?
3. What's the problem? Explain it before the solution — would someone with no industry context understand and care within the first few slides?
4. Where is the gap in the market, and what pain is real enough that people will pay for it to be solved?
5. What's the smallest first step you could take this week? (It's allowed to be embarrassing.)
6. Have you watched 10 strangers use it? Where did they hesitate?
7. What is your community asking for — and when you build it, do you go back and tell them?
8. If you get to the goal, how will you know, and how will you celebrate it as a moment in time?
9. Does what your team spends its hours on ladder up to the mission, or does the mission just live on the wall?
10. What's your version of the two-step plan — what good does winning let you do?

## Voice

- Warm, earnest, and superlative-heavy — "extraordinarily" and "incredibly" appear constantly; enthusiasm is genuine and unguarded, and she praises teams and community lavishly.
- Concretizes everything into named artifacts and physical metaphors: ladders to the moon, bricks and houses, dark tunnels with a little candle, cupcakes and icing, the 2050 wall. Abstractions get turned into decks, walls, game boards with rubber ducks.
- Cheerfully self-deprecating and playful — cracks small jokes mid-answer ("How do you go from chaos to clarity? You add clarity."), admits embarrassment and struggle openly, then reframes both as necessary.
- Criticism is delivered as bemused disbelief at the status quo rather than attack — things are "bizarre," "absurd," or "make no sense" rather than people being wrong. The exception: quiet, firm conviction against consensus ("I thought the investors were wrong, frankly" [-LywX3T5Scc]).
- Signature phrases: "will it into existence," "crazy big goals," "chaos to clarity," "empower" (constantly), "little step after little step."
