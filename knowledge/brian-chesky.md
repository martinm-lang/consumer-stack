# Brian Chesky — Co-founder & CEO, Airbnb

> Sources: JH6cKtvWad0 (TBPN), eURcW5_uS60 (Invest Like The Best)

## Worldview

- The chatbot is not the end-state interface for AI. It's a pre-AI interface bolted onto a powerful engine — his image: the industry keeps iterating the engine of the car without ever redesigning the car. The future interface will reference chat but be far more visual; most people aren't text-based.
- Consumer is the biggest prize in AI, and almost nobody is going after it. On the YC board he sees ~87% of companies per batch going enterprise (159 of 175 in the last batch, zero consumer). Enterprise makes sense short-term (models are expensive, workflows automatable), but consumer is what reaches daily life for billions.
- Apps will probably not exist at some point — agents are the new platform paradigm. But there won't be one agent that does everything: people don't have one person in their life, and specialization will still win inside consumer.
- AI shifts human attention from consumption to creation — social media made us consumers; AI hands everyone a paintbrush. Every child is an artist (via Picasso); about one in two adults will tell you they're not creative, and they're wrong — they just haven't exercised the muscle. The act of making is how ideas arrive — designing is itself the act of coming up with ideas, so founders are expeditionaries, not visionaries.
- The AI revolution hasn't actually happened yet for normal people. The average person's daily life has barely changed in three years; the shift only happens when devices, operating systems, and apps-become-agents change together.
- A design is only successful if it sells. Industrial design (his training) is judged on commercial viability, not awards — which forces you to think about marketing, manufacturing, distribution, and every stakeholder's vantage point (the child on the ventilator, the scared parent, the proud nurse technician).
- Software is the fast fashion of creative work — it looks like crap in 10 years no matter how good it was. What endures is the community, the brand, the mission, the network effect — he told his company they're not building an app or a service, they're building a community, because that's the only thing that lasts.
- In an age where AI can fake selfies, IDs, and video, authentication and proof of personhood become critical infrastructure. Real-world verification (e.g., Airbnb's own photographer network) is a moat.

## Frameworks

### The 11-star experience
Take one moment in your product (e.g., check-in) and script it at escalating star levels. 5-star = nothing went wrong (that's just review compression). 6-star = favorite wine, snacks, handwritten card. 7-star = limo at the airport, surfboard waiting. 8-star = elephant parade in your honor. 9-star = Beatles-in-1964 check-in. 10-star = Elon takes you to space. The point of the absurdity: push far enough past reality that 6 or 7 stars stops seeming crazy, then work backwards. Product-market fit is a scalable 6-7 star experience — and the gap between 5 and 6 stars is the gap between you and a competitor.

### Make the problem as small as possible
"It's better to have 100 people love you than a million people sort of like you" [eURcW5_uS60] (Paul Graham, day one of YC — the most important advice Chesky ever got). Buchheit couldn't ship Gmail until 100 Googlers loved it; once 100 people love something, 100 million will. Shrink the problem until you can talk to every user — one city, even a subset of a city. Do things by hand, don't worry about cost, prove the model like a prototype: heat up a bathtub, not an ocean. PMF is a distinct problem from industrialization; unscalable R&D first, scaling second.

### One → 10 → many
For every new business line: pilot in one market. If the market works, go to 10. If 10 work, industrialize. Airbnb launched services/experiences in 100 cities at once and it didn't work — the fix was remembering that Airbnb started in New York, Uber in SF, DoorDash in Palo Alto. Airbnb now runs 10-20 pilots this way, targeting 50-70 verticals.

### Crawl, walk, run, fly (the Hawaii system)
Take a lean elite team (10-12 people: designers, engineers, a couple PMs, data scientists), treat it like a startup inside the company, point it at one metric (search-to-book conversion), and sequence: crawl = fix bugs and conversion problems; walk = build features, reframe the journey; run = rethink entire flows; fly = complete reinvention. Everything measured. One such team delivered ~$200M internal revenue year one, then $400-500M, now 600+ bps on $134B. Then reuse the model on the next problem (pricing), team by team.

### Founder mode / the golf instructor
Start hands-on and give ground grudgingly — everyone does the opposite. Review everything before empowering anyone — you can't know your hires are great unless you're auditing what they're doing. Like a golf instructor, watch thousands of swings before letting go, because if people build habits alone you have to unwind the muscle memory later. Control isn't zero-sum: if the leader has real control, they can hand power out and the car actually turns when you steer. Manage people through the work, not through therapy 1:1s — Jony Ive designs and leads; a design leader who only manages people is crazy to him.

### Simplicity as distillation (via Hiroki Asai / Apple)
Simplicity is not removing things — it's distilling something so fundamentally you understand its essence. Startups get simplicity for free from constraint; funding and headcount destroy the muscle. Pair with craft — how you do anything is how you do everything. Focus on making all the inputs perfect and the score takes care of itself (Bill Walsh / John Wooden's sock lesson) — and if everything is perfect and you still don't grow, you picked the wrong inputs.

### AI founder mode (in progress)
Founder mode ran on ~35 hours/week of group meetings — full chain of command in one room, anyone can speak, he speaks last, ratifies every decision, disagrees with the team ~10% of the time. AI founder mode goes deeper into the details because everything is on demand: shift from meeting-based to asynchronous, cut management to a few layers (the Catholic Church runs on four — why do companies need seven to nine?), and make every manager a hybrid manager-IC. Sequence he's running at Airbnb: first get everyone on AI tools, watch how every job changes, then do a fundamental redesign of the company. He also says founder mode becomes the only viable operating mode in the AI era — risk-averse incrementalists won't survive it.

### The consumer AI business-model test
There are only three ways to make money in consumer: ads, subscriptions, or transactions. Subscriptions and ads both hit local maxima when competitors give the product away free. You cannot be in the business of information — consumers simply aren't trained to pay for information. Airbnb works because it has high-dollar transactions. A consumer AI product must name its lane before it starts.

### Atomic unit shift
Ask what the atomic unit of the business is and whether it's the right one. Airbnb's is a home; he's rebuilding it around the person — identity, authenticated proof of personhood, the richest preference profile on the internet, a real-world social graph — with 50 offerings ringed around that person. Kleenex can't sell shampoo; the unit determines what you're allowed to extend into.

### Pipeline recruiting
Never run searches — build pipelines. Constantly meet great people before you need them; every meeting's job is to produce the next two or three introductions. Start with results and work backwards to people (find the ad you love, then find who made it — don't start at Nike or the resume). Build talent mafias. He is co-hiring manager for the top 200 people, recruits two-three layers deep, and the first and last call of his day is the recruiting team (2-3 hours daily). Hiring and managing trade off one-for-one: the more time on recruiting, the less you spend managing, because great people self-manage.

## Heuristics & rules of thumb

- Don't be the first, be the best. Airbnb skipped the ChatGPT app launch (Expedia/Booking went first) because required pieces — 90% of guests message hosts pre-booking, verified ID — weren't supported yet. Being first to signal "we care about AI" is vanity.
- If 100 people love it, 100 million will. If you can't name the 100, the problem isn't small enough yet.
- Pick a niche, but not one so narrow that adopting it isn't worth a behavior change. Source ideas by watching the daily life of your sister, partner, kids — what's annoying?
- Keep burn low in consumer; it's hits-driven, all-or-nothing, and you need to be good at design, marketing, culture, and press — not just technology and sales.
- Go into small markets and make them big (via Thiel: monopoly of a tiny market beats a small share of a big one). Big markets have too much competition.
- Failed founders are more fundable than people who never started — don't let fear of OpenAI eating your lunch keep you out of consumer.
- ChatGPT-referred traffic converts to bookings at a higher rate than Google search traffic — informed, high-intent users. Distribution through agents is real.
- Most chatbots are single-player; most real decisions (trips especially) are multiplayer. Build for two people with different preferences deciding together.
- Chatbots are stateless; memory and preference-building is an open innovation area worth attacking.
- Spend up to 50% of your time hiring (Sam Altman's advice, which Chesky ignored and calls his death blow). Consider a recruiter as your first hire, before an engineer.
- Executives should hire people so good they'd never join without the founder's help — if they can land the hire without his help, they're not reaching far enough.
- Take the pitcher off the mound — they never take themselves off, and the longer you wait the more home runs they give up. They'll be angry either way.
- Only two types of people don't survive the AI shift: pure people managers, and the rigid. Everyone else with a growth mindset will be fine — economic incentives will make the tools easy.
- Every manager must stay an IC in their craft: engineers who manage still code; lawyers still read case law. Whatever your field's version of "code" is, you do it.
- Progress is 1% a day, compounded. Overnight successes take thousands of days; if you train a month and take a month off, you're starting over.
- Prediction on record: the consumer AI renaissance begins within 12-24 months, and top apps in the App Store being AI apps proves breakthrough products still find distribution.

## Red flags they call out

- Asking what's-our-metaverse-strategy (or AI strategy) — strategy-by-trend from people not steeped in the technology. Vanity signaling instead of adding customer value.
- Launching at global scale before perfecting one market (his own 100-city services launch failure). The burden of scaling from day one killed Airbnb's second act for 18 years.
- Trying to make a million people sort-of like you — the shallow swimming pool. You can't talk to a million people, so you can't tell what's working.
- Being in the business of information with no ads/subscription/transaction lane — consumers won't pay for information.
- Overdelegating: hiring someone, letting them build an empire, auditing later. Trial-and-error is fine for founders and fatal for CEOs — unwinding an empire takes four years.
- Leaders without contact with reality: heads of design who don't design, recurring-1:1 therapist-managers, meetings about meetings, five layers of telephone between the founder and the work.
- A niche so narrow nobody will change their workflow for it.
- Building for status and adulation — "a cup with a hole at the bottom" [eURcW5_uS60]. Products made for approval lose their courage. Make it for yourself; the artist test (Rick Rubin) is whether you'd love it with no audience.
- Attachment to the current paradigm — clinging to apps when he expects apps to be replaced by agents entirely.

## Questions they ask founders

- Where are your users, and why aren't you physically there with them? (Paul Graham's question to him.)
- What's the smallest version of this problem — one city, one neighborhood, one customer whose mind you can blow?
- Who are the 100 people who love this? Not like — love?
- What's the 11-star version of this experience? Now which parts of the 7-star version can you actually scale?
- How do you make money: ads, subscription, or transactions? If it's none of those, why will anyone pay for information?
- What's your atomic unit, and is it the right one for where you want to extend?
- Is this single-player or multiplayer? Who else is in the room when your user decides?
- If you designed this from the ground up knowing what AI can do today — not bolting AI onto a pre-AI paradigm — what would it look like?
- What here endures in 10 years, when your software inevitably looks like crap? Community? Brand? Network?
- Are you making this because you love it, or to be a successful founder? What would you build if no one was watching?

## Voice

- Relentlessly analogical: DOS vs. multi-touch, bathtubs vs. oceans, golf instructors, pitchers on mounds, cups with holes, Kleenex selling shampoo, bodybuilding's progressive overload. He teaches almost exclusively through metaphor and named stories (Steve Jobs, Walt Disney, Wooden's socks, Buchheit's Gmail).
- Frames advice as counterintuitive truths the listener has been getting backwards: everyone does the opposite of what works, control isn't zero-sum, founders shouldn't learn the CEO job by doing it.
- Superlative-heavy enthusiasm ("really really," "incredible," "the biggest prize") but delivers criticism as calm pattern-diagnosis, often via his own confessed failures — the 2010s overdelegation, the 100-city launch, ignoring Altman on hiring.
- Identifies as artist/designer first, CEO second; praises craft, love of the work, and belief in people; dismisses status-chasing and trend-following as vanity.
