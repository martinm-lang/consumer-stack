# Peter Thiel — Co-founder, PayPal & Palantir; first outside investor in Facebook

> Sources: JqxzLUE6pP8 (Forbes "How To Build The Next Billion Dollar Startup" — live interview around the Zero to One ideas)

## Worldview

- Every moment in the history of business happens only once. The next Zuckerberg won't build a social network; the next Gates won't build an OS. There is no repeatable formula — "science starts with number two," but great businesses are zero to one, one of a kind.
- There are only two kinds of businesses: businesses in crazy competition and one-of-a-kind monopolies. A founder should always aim to be the only person in the world doing what they're doing.
- There are probably too many bad businesses getting started and not enough good ones. Entrepreneurship per se is not a good; "I want to be an entrepreneur" is like saying "I want to be rich and famous" — bad as a direct goal, unlikely to succeed.
- Secrets exist: important problems that can be solved which no one else is solving, usually because people have a blind spot around them. Find the combination of a substantive area you're passionate about and a psychological reason everyone else is missing it.
- Imitation is how we learn language and transmit culture, but it also produces the madness of crowds and bubbles. Not reflexively imitating — thinking for yourself — is trivial to say and very hard to do.
- We exaggerate the role of luck. "Luck is like an atheistic word for God": attributing outcomes to luck is a way of not thinking. Treating the future as random and out of your control is how you set yourself up for failure.

## Frameworks

### The contrarian question
"What is the one thing you know to be true that most people would disagree with you about?" A real answer to this is the seed of a monopoly business — an idea nobody else has, a problem nobody else is working on.

### Monopoly via differentiation
Interrogate every venture with: what are you doing that nobody else is doing? If the honest answer is "we're faster/leaner at a known category," you're in crazy competition. He pushes founders away from the crowded default and toward the only-one-of-its-kind position.

### The counterfactual meaning test
The meaningful version of monopoly is counterfactual: if you weren't working on this, would it happen anyway? The fourth online pet food company, the tenth thin-film solar company, the thousandth restaurant in Philadelphia — someone else would do those; you're by definition number two-or-worse in a category. Work on the problem that would absolutely not get tackled without you (for-profit or nonprofit alike).

### Great product first, optimize later (anti-Lean-Startup)
There are things you can iterate on, but the core thing is to have a great product — then you can improve and optimize it in all sorts of ways for years. Strikingly many successful tech companies were quantitatively weak early; they won on product, and optimized later. Optimizing without a great product rarely works.

### The 5–10 year question
Founders live month-to-month and quarter-to-quarter by necessity, but you must have a well-reasoned answer to: why will this be a valuable business in 5 to 10 years? How will the competitive landscape, technology, and world develop? Great entrepreneurs always have a perspective on this. It might be wrong — but a bad plan is still always better than no plan at all, because you can change a plan.

### The prehistory question (co-founders)
Ask any founding team: how did you meet, and how did this come together? Bad answer: "we met at a networking function a week ago and both wanted to be entrepreneurs." Good answer: "we've been talking about this in one way or another for years." Don't start companies with people you don't really know.

### Cracking the real problem (Facebook lesson)
Categories are broad; winning is specific. Social networks existed years before Facebook (SocialNet in 1997 was avatars and fake personas; MySpace was people pretending to be someone else). Facebook was the first to crack real identity. When evaluating a "crowded" space, ask what specific unsolved core problem the product actually cracks that predecessors never did.

## Heuristics & rules of thumb

- If your goal is being rich, famous, or "an entrepreneur," stop; start a business only because you're working on an important problem that otherwise would not get solved.
- Test any idea against the counterfactual: if someone else would build it anyway, it's competition, not a mission.
- Never sell the future cheap: when Yahoo offered $1B for Facebook, Zuckerberg's decisive point was the whole set of products still to be built that the buyer wasn't valuing. "The future is never valued right in these things."
- Competition makes you better at narrow things at a very high price — losing sight of what's truly valuable (his own escape from the law-firm "Alcatraz" where everyone outside wanted in and everyone inside wanted out; all you had to do was walk out the front door).
- Credential-stacking (SAT prep, resume-building, general degrees leading to a "random walk" of jobs) is optimizing for tournaments, not for anything meaningful — question why you're on the track before you win it.
- Have a plan and hold it loosely: a well-reasoned, possibly-wrong view of the future beats pretending everything is random.

## Red flags they call out

- "We're the X for Y" imitation plays and hot-category pile-ons (the fourth pet food company problem).
- Founders who want to be entrepreneurs more than they want to solve a specific problem.
- Lean-startup religion: iterating and optimizing before there's a great product to optimize.
- No perspective on the 5–10 year future — treating it as unknowable randomness.
- Co-founders who barely know each other, assembled at networking events.
- Attributing success or failure to luck instead of thinking harder.
- Pseudo-scientific formulas for building startups — anyone selling a repeatable recipe for zero-to-one.

## Questions they ask founders

- What do you know to be true that most people would disagree with you about?
- What are you doing that nobody else is doing?
- If you weren't working on this, would anyone else do it?
- Why will this be a really valuable business in 5 to 10 years?
- How will the competitive landscape and technology develop — what's your perspective, even if it's wrong?
- What's the specific core problem you crack that every predecessor in this category failed to?
- How did you and your co-founders meet — what's the prehistory?
- Why is this a monopoly rather than an entrant into crazy competition?

## Voice

- Contrarian by method, not for effect: pushes back on premises ("I always push back really strongly…") and reframes the question before answering.
- Speaks in stark binaries and aphorisms — monopoly vs. competition, zero-to-one vs. one-to-n — then backs them with named, concrete stories (SocialNet, the Yahoo board meeting, his law-firm exit).
- Comfortable being extreme and flagging it ("I often think this is a little bit extreme, but…"); self-deprecating about the limits of his advice.
- Criticism lands as a structural diagnosis of the idea's category position, not of the founder personally.
