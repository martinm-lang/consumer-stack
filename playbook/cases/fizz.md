# Fizz

> **Era:** 2021–present | **Wedge:** one dorm at Stanford | **Atomic network:** a single campus, `.edu`-gated
> **Status:** live, ~250–300 campuses, >$40M raised, founder-led since Oct 2024
> **Sources:** 4 primary interviews (transcripts), Stanford McCoy Center case study w/ primary citations, TechCrunch ×3, Stanford Daily, 5 university newspapers.

## TL;DR

Two Stanford freshmen who never got a freshman year built an anonymous `.edu`-gated campus feed, prototyped it quietly at **Cornell and University of Chicago**, then launched it at Stanford with a 6 a.m. flyer drop under dorm doors executed by ~20 recruited friends. The campus became the product: one `.edu` domain = one closed network, with paid local student moderators who both police *and seed* the content. They then productized the launch itself — "launch leader ambassadors," donuts, branded hats, three Zoom training calls — and ran it campus by campus, deliberately pacing to ~250 campuses rather than the 1,000 they publicly targeted. The machine's real innovation is not the flyers; it is **making moderation local and paid**, which is the fix for the Yik Yak failure mode that killed the previous generation of anonymous campus apps.

**The single most important finding in this file:** the founder's own retelling ("the whole school got on it" that day) is contradicted by the contemporaneous record (~10% in week one). See *Metrics* — the gap matters more than either number.

## The machine

> Stanford dorms (wedge) → one `.edu` domain (atomic network) → flyer swarm + paid moderators who seed posts (cold start) → ambassador-run launch ops, donuts, campus meme pages (distribution) → **no true viral loop; the loop is operational, not product-borne** → anonymous local gossip + marketplace + events (retention) → `.edu` exclusivity makes saturation legible (saturation) → next campus chosen by inbound request volume → "Global Fizz" attempts the escape to non-campus life

## Initial conditions

**The problem was manufactured by COVID and it was real.** An entire class arrived at a university they had never physically attended. The specific artifact Solomon names is a **1,200-person Stanford class GroupMe in which almost nobody spoke** — a network with maximal membership and zero throughput.

> "We got put into a GroupMe with 1,200 students, where four people talked probably, and we were like, this doesn't work." — Solomon, via TechCrunch

That is the sharpest statement of the wedge available. The addressable group was already assembled and already failing. Fizz did not have to create a network; it had to make an existing one usable.

**Why the network was dense:** a residential campus is the densest social unit in American life outside a prison or a ship. Everyone is within walking distance, shares a schedule, shares physical referents (dining halls, dorms, a specific bad professor), and turns over 25% per year — creating permanent renewed demand. **[Interpretation]**

**Why 10x in this context specifically:** the alternatives were Instagram (a highlight reel), a dead 1,200-person GroupMe (no anonymity, no filtering, no persistence), and Reddit (not local, not gated). Fizz's stated thesis is the negative space:

> "Build a platform for the 99% of life not on Instagram and TikTok." — Solomon [Yh-lv_Z3pmU]

Anonymity is what makes the 99% postable. You cannot post "I just failed this exam, is anyone else in the same boat" under your own name to people you will see at breakfast.

## First users

**First ~10 — Cornell and Chicago, not Stanford.** [Confirmed — McCoy]
The founders prototyped on two campuses they had no personal network in, before launching on the one they did. **[Interpretation]** This inverts the usual advice: they spent their *weakest* networks on learning, and their *strongest* network on the launch that had to work. Most founders burn the home campus on version one.

**First ~700 — Stanford, week one, July 29 2021.** ~10% of undergrads. [Confirmed]
Channel: a physical flyer drop, described by Solomon:

> "At 6:00 in the morning me and my friends just went around campus and threw flyers at people doors." [xO2SnWG-Li0]

The operation, in his own account: he called ~10 friends and told them to stand outside a dorm, called 10 more and told them to open their dorm's door, and the group pushed flyers under individual room doors. Roughly 1,000–2,000 members of his class were physically on campus that summer morning. His stated expectation was ~100 downloads — his friends plus one hop.

**First ~5,700 — Stanford, months 6–12.** ~75% by early 2022, ~95% by mid-2022. [Company-reported]
Note the shape: **the flyer drop produced 10%; the remaining 85 points took a year.** The launch tactic bought the seed. Something else — anonymity, local content, the fall term restarting in person — did the saturating.

**Campuses 2 and 3 — Pepperdine, then Chapman, days apart.** Solomon flew down, recruited students in person by asking how the app fit *their* campus story, launched, and watched it repeat. Chapman is the moment the thesis generalized:

> "Then it took over all of Chapman and then we really knew we had not solved the Stanford problem." [xO2SnWG-Li0]

**Campuses 4–15 — the fall 2022 cash sprint.** This is the most instructive stretch and the least reported. The market had closed, competitors existed, and Fizz needed a Series A. Solomon put his Stanford roommate on a plane, sent two more friends to other schools, and personally launched Rice, Wake Forest, Elon, Yale, Princeton, Tulane and Dartmouth back to back. Before leaving he wrote down his estimated probability of success at each school and multiplied them:

> "It was like 8% or something like that." [xO2SnWG-Li0]

They hit essentially all of them. **[Interpretation]** The 8% was wrong because he modeled the launches as independent events. They were not: the same operational playbook, run by the same people, on structurally identical networks, is one experiment repeated — correlated, not independent. **Estimating a campus-by-campus rollout as a product of independent probabilities will always look impossible and will always be wrong in the same direction.**

## Cold start

Fizz solved the empty-room problem three ways, and only one of them is usually discussed.

**1. Temporal compression.** Everyone gets the flyer in the same hour of the same morning. First openers find other people already posting because everyone arrived simultaneously. **[Interpretation]** Compression converts a trickle into a room.

**2. Physical density as free frequency.** A flyer under a dorm door is seen once, but the *conversation about it* runs all day through people who live together. This is the cheap version of Bier's "users need to see the message ~3 times": on a residential campus, the network does repetition for you. **[Interpretation]**

**3. Paid moderators who generate the content.** This is the underreported mechanism and the ethically loaded one:

> Fizz "would recruit moderators to review user-generated posts **and generate posts themselves to jump-start the platform's context-specific content**." [Confirmed — McCoy case study]

Moderators were recruited via LinkedIn and in-app, trained, and compensated inconsistently: **volunteer at Occidental, ~$500/month at Amherst and Bates.** Amherst had ~15 student moderators within weeks of launch. [Confirmed — Amherst Student, Occidental]

So the first content a student saw on a fresh campus was partly written by paid students hired to write it. **This is semi-artificial activity.** It was not disclosed in the product.

**Is it reproducible in 2026?** Mostly yes, and it is cheap — 15 students × $500 is $7,500/month per campus, less than a week of paid acquisition for the same 5,000 people. **[Interpretation]** The exposure is disclosure: a paid seeder posting as an anonymous peer is a materially different thing from a moderator, and the two roles were held by the same person.

## Launch mechanism

The launch was productized into a repeatable operation, and the details are unusually well documented:

- **Launch leader ambassadors.** Recruited by cold email, required to attend **three Zoom training meetings**, and — critically — **responsible for recruiting the other ambassadors** themselves. [Confirmed — Occidental] The ambassador program is itself a referral tree; Fizz recruited one person per campus and made hiring the rest that person's job.
- **Donuts and branded merch** (hats) traded for downloads. [Confirmed]
- **Borrowed owned media:** at Seattle University, Fizz approached **existing campus meme pages** rather than building its own following. [Confirmed — Seattle Spectator]
- **Never through the administration.** Solomon is explicit: "we don't go through the administration, we go through the students." [xO2SnWG-Li0] Universities cannot approve an anonymous gossip app; asking creates a veto point that does not otherwise exist. **[Interpretation]**
- **Result on launch day:** "hundreds of students downloading the app within the first day" on some campuses — with the honest caveat, from the same source, that **many downloaded and stayed inactive or deleted it.** [Confirmed — McCoy]
- **Deliberate pacing.** Cofer: "We could have easily expanded this to 500 campuses a year ago, but we deliberately chose not to." [Stanford Daily] Against a publicly stated Oct 2022 target of **1,000 campuses by end of 2023**, actual was ~80 in Aug 2023 and ~250 by Oct 2024. Read the pacing quote against the target: this is a **miss reframed as restraint.** Both may be true; only one was said in advance.
- **The operation was retired.** By 2024–25: "nowadays we don't even do that operation." Inbound school requests — "tens of thousands" [Company-reported] — replaced outbound launches.

That last point is the whole lesson, and it matches Nikita Bier's insistence that school seeding is a **testing** strategy, not a growth strategy (`knowledge/nikita-bier.md`). Fizz used it as a *bootstrapping* strategy and then discarded it, which is a third thing: seeding as a way to buy time until inbound demand exists.

## Viral loop

**Fizz does not have a strong product-borne viral loop, and this is the most important negative finding in the file.**

Write it out and the missing edge is obvious:

> User A posts anonymously → User B reads it **inside the app** → B was already a user → nothing leaves the network

There is no artifact. Nothing is sent to a non-user. Anonymity actively forbids the two things that usually carry a social product outward: **you cannot tag anyone, and you cannot take credit.** A Fizz post cannot be screenshotted into a group chat as *yours*.

The observed spread mechanism is therefore **word of mouth about the app**, not distribution *by* the app — students telling each other in person that something funny is on Fizz. That works at 10,000 people on 8,000 acres and does not survive contact with a dispersed population.

**[Interpretation]** This is why Fizz's growth is operationally expensive (ambassadors, flyers, donuts, paid moderators, founders on planes) while Venmo's or Partiful's is not. Fizz buys each network; a loop-borne product is given the next one by the last one. It is also why "Global Fizz" is hard: strip the campus and you remove the only thing doing the distributing.

**What Fizz has instead of a loop:** `.edu` gating creates a **completeness incentive**. If 95% of your campus is on it and the conversation about your dorm is happening there, non-participation costs you information. That is closer to a *utility lock-in* than to virality, and it only switches on after saturation — which is why the first 10% has to be bought.

## Social graph mechanics

Fizz has **no friend graph at all.** There is no contact sync, no username exchange, no follow. Membership in the network is established by one credential — a valid `@school.edu` address — and every member of the network sees the same feed.

This is a genuinely different design point and worth naming: **the graph is the geography.** Identity is "person at this school," not "person I know." Consequences:

- Onboarding has no cold-start friend problem. There is no "you have 0 friends" state. Time-to-value is the time to read one funny post.
- Bans are enforceable and durable, because identity is anchored to an institution-issued credential the user cannot cheaply reissue. [Confirmed — TechCrunch]
- Users cannot follow the network when they leave it. **Graduation is total churn.** The `.edu` gate that makes the network dense also guarantees 100% of every cohort is expelled from it within four years. Fizz's entire long-term problem is contained in this line.

## Retention loop

- **Anonymous local gossip**, refreshed constantly by a population that shares physical referents. "Fizzin'" surfaces the most popular posts of the last 12 hours — a rolling, half-day content clock. [Confirmed]
- **Creation ratio: over one-third of users create content.** [Founder-reported] If true this is extraordinary — Alex Zhu called 25% "extremely high" for Musical.ly (`knowledge/alex-zhu.md`). Anonymity plausibly explains it: the cost of posting a bad post is zero when nothing attaches to you. **[Interpretation]**
- **Utility layers added on top of gossip:** peer-to-peer marketplace (2024), events, class discussion, housing. This is the durable half. **[Interpretation]** Gossip retains a campus for a term; "where do I sell my bike" retains it for four years.

### The narrative gap — read this before quoting any Fizz number

Solomon's account of launch day: *"the whole school got on it, just blew our minds."* [xO2SnWG-Li0]
The contemporaneous record: **700+ users, ~10% of undergrads, after a week.** [Stanford Daily, Aug 2021]

Both describe the same event. The founder's version is not a lie — 700 people appearing overnight in a dead 1,200-person class chat genuinely *feels* like the whole school, and by the time he is telling the story the 95% figure is true. But the two numbers describe different things separated by roughly a year, and the retelling has silently merged them.

**[Transferable]** Founder launch anecdotes compress time. The emotional memory is of the moment saturation felt inevitable; the number attached to it is usually the terminal number, not the day-one number. When a founder tells you a school "all got on it in a day," the operational question to ask is *what fraction, measured when* — because the honest answer (~10% in week one) is the one you can actually plan a launch against, and the 95% is a year of compounding you cannot copy.

This one correction changes what a campus launch should be budgeted for: **a good launch day buys ~10%, not a campus.**

## Expansion

- **Geographic:** campus by campus, chosen initially by founder reach (Pepperdine, Chapman — both drivable from Palo Alto), later by inbound request volume. Note the early sequence favors *proximity*, not prestige.
- **Demographic:** attempted **downward** into high schools and **retreated** — Fizz exited the high-school market entirely after bullying incidents at a Vermont high school in 2023. [Confirmed] A rare, clean, documented case of a company abandoning an adjacent market on safety grounds.
- **Use-case:** gossip → marketplace → events → advertising. The marketplace is the strategically serious move: it competes with Facebook Marketplace on a turf Facebook has abandoned, and it is the first Fizz feature with a reason to exist after graduation. **[Interpretation]**
- **Escape attempt:** "Global Fizz" — experience- and identity-based communities beyond the `.edu` gate, with a nearby-content layer. Unproven. This is the Facebook-1.0 problem (see `cases/` for Facebook when written): the gate that created the density is the thing you must eventually remove, and removing it is not symmetric with adding it. **[Interpretation]**

## What stopped working

- **The manual launch operation.** Retired once inbound demand existed. It never scaled to the stated 1,000 campuses; it got to ~250 with a large ops team and a lot of flying.
- **The 1,000-campus target.** Publicly stated Oct 2022 for end of 2023; missed by ~92%.
- **High schools.** Abandoned.
- **The seed's own half-life.** The McCoy case study is blunt that many launch-day downloads went inactive or were deleted. Download counts on launch day are the vainest number in campus growth. **[Interpretation]**

## Ethical & safety considerations

This section is longer than the equivalent for most companies because the record is unusually well documented by student journalists, and because the master brief asks specifically what should *not* be copied.

- **Paid seeding without disclosure.** Moderators were paid to generate posts that appeared as ordinary anonymous student content. Combining "person who writes the content" with "person who decides which content stays up" in one paid role is a conflict, and neither half was disclosed to users. **Do not copy this.** The defensible version is a clearly labeled account or a founder posting under their own name.
- **Documented harms.** Student newspapers across many campuses — Amherst, GW, Wake Forest, NC Central, Seton Hall, Claremont, Fairfield, Colby — report bullying, harassment, racially-motivated hate speech (posts targeting Asian and Filipino students at NCCU), and reputational damage to named individuals. Two documented cases: a Student Government Association candidate and a first-year running for a Residence Hall Association seat, both of whom described reading anonymous posts about themselves as "alarming" and lastingly damaging. [Confirmed — GW Hatchet, Campus Echo]
- **Non-users are exposed.** A student who never downloads Fizz can still be discussed by name on it. Consent to the network is not required to be a subject of it.
- **The structural bet:** Fizz's answer to Yik Yak is **local paid human moderation** rather than crowd downvoting — the McCoy study identifies this as the substantive design difference, and Fizz claims sub-one-minute average removal. [Company-reported] The bet is real and is better than Yik Yak's. The student-press record says it is not sufficient.
- **Nikita Bier's rule applies directly and Fizz fails it.** He refuses to build anonymous free-text messaging because it "reliably ends in bullying and tragedy," and argues you should *constrain the input* so the product can only produce good outcomes (`knowledge/nikita-bier.md`). Fizz constrained the *audience* (`.edu`) and moderated the *output*, but left the input unconstrained. Every documented harm above lives in that gap.

## Transferable principles

1. **[Transferable] A good campus launch day buys ~10%, not a campus.** Budget the other 90% as a year of compounding, and pick tactics accordingly.
2. **[Transferable] Prototype on a campus you don't care about; launch on the one you do.** Fizz burned Cornell and Chicago to learn and spent Stanford once.
3. **[Transferable] Temporal compression is the cheapest cold-start tool available.** Same hour, same morning, whole population. It converts a trickle into a populated room at zero marginal cost.
4. **[Transferable] Make the ambassador's job be recruiting ambassadors.** One cold-emailed student per campus, trained on three calls, hiring the rest — the ops layer is itself a referral tree.
5. **[Transferable] Go through students, never the administration.** Institutional approval is a veto you are not otherwise subject to.
6. **[Transferable] Local, paid, in-community moderation is a defensible answer to the anonymous-app death spiral** — necessary, demonstrably not sufficient, and it must not be the same headcount that seeds content.
7. **[Transferable] Gating on an institutional credential gives you enforceable bans and legible saturation, and charges you 100% cohort churn for it.** Know which side of that trade your product is on before you build the gate.
8. **[Transferable] Anonymity buys creation rate and forfeits virality.** >1/3 creator ratio and no shareable artifact are the same design decision. Choose deliberately; you cannot have both.
9. **[Transferable] Never model a rollout as independent probabilities.** Fizz's own founder computed 8% and was wrong, because repeating one playbook on structurally identical networks is one correlated experiment.

## What Zimo can test

All of the following are **[Zimo hypothesis]** — proposed experiments, not findings.

- **[Zimo hypothesis] Steal the compression, skip the anonymity.** Fizz's transferable asset is the 6 a.m. simultaneous drop into a residential population, not the anonymous feed. Zimo's money-coordination object is inherently attributed, so it keeps the artifact Fizz forfeited. Test: one dorm, one morning, one flyer whose call to action is a *live split someone already owes you money in* — not an app download.
- **[Zimo hypothesis] Fizz's missing edge is Zimo's whole product.** A Fizz post reaches no non-user; a Zimo split *must* reach a non-user by construction (the person who owes you). Zimo should never need paid content seeders, because the first user's own action populates the second user's experience. If Zimo finds itself hiring seeders, the loop is broken — treat that as a diagnostic.
- **[Zimo hypothesis] Test the 10% bar explicitly.** Set launch-day success at ~10% of a residential population *transacting* (not downloading), matching Fizz's real week-one number rather than its narrative one. Fizz's own data says download-day counts are inflated by immediate churn.
- **[Zimo hypothesis] The marketplace is the tell.** Fizz's most durable feature is peer-to-peer campus buying and selling — students already move money to each other constantly and are doing it in a gossip app. That is latent demand in Bier's exact sense: a distorted process wrapping a real motivation. Zimo should look at what Fizz Marketplace posts settle payment with today.
- **[Zimo hypothesis] Solve graduation, which Fizz cannot.** `.edu` gating dooms Fizz to expelling every user within four years. Zimo's campus wedge must not be `.edu`-gated for exactly this reason — the roommate group that graduates is still a roommate group, and money coordination survives the move to a city. Use the campus for density, never for identity.
- **[Zimo hypothesis] Buy the ambassador tree, not the ambassadors.** Recruit one launch leader per campus by cold email, pay them to recruit the rest, and measure cost-per-*activated-group* rather than cost-per-download.
